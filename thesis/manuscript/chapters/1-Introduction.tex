\chapter{Introduction}\label{chapter:introduction}

This work investigates how to train an object detector small enough for embedded hardware when real training images are scarce. Wildlife species recognition is used as the test case. The deployment context is a consumer binocular that classifies the mammal a user is looking at, on the device itself, and without a network connection. That fixes two hard constraints: a per-frame latency budget on a mobile system-on-chip, and a class universe of $225$ mammal species rather than the few dozen coarse classes of a general detection benchmark. The detector must locate the animal and classify its species. Classification is difficult because many of these species differ only in fine markings (\Cref{sec:results_granularity_gaps}). Two training strategies are compared: distilling a much larger fine-tuned teacher model into a small student model, and fine-tuning the same student model directly on the wildlife dataset. Of the $225$ classes, $50$ retain fewer than $150$ usable real photographs each, so this work also asks whether diffusion-generated imagery can provide the missing real data, and how far a detector trained on it still transfers to real photographs.

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

Species identification in the field has begun to move from printed field guides into the optics themselves. The \textit{AX Visio} binocular \cite{AXVisio} runs a species-recognition model on board and classifies the animal the user is already looking at. Classification must occur while the animal remains within the field of view. This rules out sending the frame to a remote service. The habitats in which the device is used also preclude this option, since network coverage cannot be assumed there. Therefore, the recognition model must fit on a mobile system-on-chip and meet its per-frame latency budget. The deployment target is the \textit{Qualcomm QCS605}, a mobile platform that combines a \textit{Hexagon 685} digital signal processor and an \textit{Adreno 615} graphics processor \cite{QCS605}. However, the compute budget of this platform excludes the model scales at which detection accuracy is typically reported. For software-maturity reasons set out in \Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}, development and benchmarking in this work targeted a \textit{Raspberry Pi 400} instead \cite{ltdBuyRaspberryPi}.

Accurate detection comes at the cost of model size and inference speed. The most accurate models are large \cite{zouObjectDetection202023}, which makes an embedded target difficult to achieve. The teacher model used in this study has approximately $193\,\mathrm{M}$ parameters, compared to $2.71\,\mathrm{M}$ for the student model that was actually exported to the device. Knowledge distillation is the established solution to such a discrepancy. Early work on model compression trained a small, fast model to reproduce the outputs of a large ensemble \cite{buciluaModelCompression2006}. Later work generalized the idea by having the student match the teacher's softened output distribution rather than its hard predictions \cite{hintonDistillingKnowledgeNeural2015}. Whether that mechanism survives a move from general object classes to fine-grained species is the question this work was built around.

Two properties of the wildlife domain make that question non-trivial. The class universe is fine-grained: many of the $225$ classes differ only in fine markings, and a detector has to learn exactly those markings \cite{weiFineGrainedImageAnalysis2022}. The available imagery is long-tailed, with a few well-photographed species dominating the corpus while most are supported by comparatively few usable photographs \cite{zhangDeepLongTailedLearning2023}. Machine-learning work on wildlife imagery treats both properties as recurring obstacles rather than as edge cases \cite{tuiaPerspectivesMachineLearning2022}. Camera-trap detection is also known to degrade sharply once a model is evaluated at locations it was not trained on \cite{beeryRecognitionTerraIncognita2018}, so a thin per-class image pool is a generalization problem and not only a sample-count problem.

Text-to-image diffusion models \cite{rombachHighResolutionImageSynthesis2022} offer the one intervention that can add appearance content a data-poor class's own photographs do not contain, such as an unrecorded pose, habitat or lighting condition. It is suggested, that a classifier trained from scratch on generated images may need roughly five times as many of them to match real images of the same classes \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023}, and prompting an off-the-shelf generator with bare class names produced a very poor classifier \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. Earlier work by the present author found that generated image context conferred no downstream detection advantage over per-pixel random noise on a small-object car-detection task \cite{hedrichGeneratingSyntheticDatasets2026}, which is what leaves the choice of generator an open question here (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison}). This work therefore treats synthetic imagery as a supplement whose usefulness is measured rather than assumed.

Recognizing wildlife species with a handheld optic presents two problems at once, making it a demanding test case. It combines a fixed latency ceiling, a fine-grained class universe, a long-tailed image supply, and a product requirement that the resulting weights remain commercially licensable. None of these constraints were created for the experiment.

\section{Objective}\label{sec:objective}

The objective is to build, train and evaluate a nano-scale detector for wildlife species recognition on embedded hardware, and to measure which of the available training strategies actually helps it. Nano-scale here means the smallest variants of a detector family, at roughly one to three million parameters. Detection accuracy is the mean average precision over species classes, scoring both the box and species together. Thus, a correctly located animal with the wrong species name is counted as a miss. It is reported throughout on the mixed test set, the union of the real and synthetic held-out images, with the real test set breakout stated alongside it every time (\Cref{sec:mixed_default}). Four research questions organize the work:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does knowledge distillation from a fine-tuned teacher model into a nano-scale student model yield higher detection accuracy than fine-tuning the same student directly on the wildlife dataset, given the domain shift from COCO-style classes to $225$ mammal species?
    \item Which synthetic-image generator produces the most useful training data for this domain, and do the two criteria available before any detector is trained, apparent visual realism and per-image price, predict that ranking?
    \item How does the composition of a class's training data, from synthetic images alone through a large pool of real photographs, affect detection accuracy, and does accuracy measured on the mixed test set hold up on the real test set alone?
    \item Does a nano-scale detector meet the per-frame latency budget of the target hardware without quantization or any other compression step, measured on a target-adjacent device?
\end{enumerate}

The four questions did not receive equal effort. Dataset construction and synthetic image generation took considerably more time than initially allocated, so questions $2$ and $3$ represent most of the experimental work. Question $1$ was answered using data from a single teacher-student pairing and a single distillation hyperparameter setting. Question $4$ was answered using a single full-precision runtime measurement on the proxy device. \Cref{sec:limitations} treats this reduced scope as a named limitation. \Cref{chapter:discussion_conclusion} restates and answers each question in turn, using the evidence assembled in \Cref{chapter:results}.

\section{Environment}\label{sec:environment}
This Master's thesis is carried out in partnership with \textit{inovex GmbH} \cite{inovex}, an IT consultancy headquartered in Karlsruhe, Germany. \textit{inovex} maintains practice areas spanning \textit{Application Development}, \textit{IT Operations}, and \textit{Data Management \& Analytics}, and the present work is hosted within the latter. The collaboration grants access to \textit{inovex}'s internal compute infrastructure, whose GPU resources make the training and distillation experiments feasible within the available timeframe. \textit{inovex} placed no fixed requirements on the research direction, leaving the choice and design of the technical approach to the author.

The application context comes from \textit{Swarovski Optik}, the Austrian optics manufacturer with which \textit{inovex} cooperates on the machine learning behind the \textit{AX Visio}.
The product owner of the AX Visio took part in the framing of this work and supplied three of its starting points: the initial corpus of images labeled with the \textit{SpeciesNet} pipeline (\Cref{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy}), the \textit{GBIF} observation counts behind the species selection, and example stills recorded with the device. Swarovski Optik also set the product requirements that \Cref{sec:pre_work} records.

\paragraph{The AX Visio}
The \textit{AX Visio} is a $10\times32$ binocular that combines conventional optics with a $13$-megapixel camera, a mobile system-on-chip, and a display projected into the eyepiece \cite{AXVisio}. The user selects an identification mode on a dial, centers the animal in a frame shown in the field of view, and presses a button. The camera then records a still, the on-device model processes it, and the species name appears in the eyepiece. Two identification modes share this mechanism but not their models. \textit{Bird ID} is built on the \textit{Merlin Bird ID} algorithm of the Cornell Lab of Ornithology \cite{AXVisio}, whose photo model covers more than $8{,}000$ bird species \cite{IdentifyBirdsPhotos}.
\textit{Mammal ID} derives from Swarovski Optik's own \textit{Wildlife ID} application and covers roughly $300$ mammal species of Europe and North America \cite{HowDoesSWAROVSKI}.
This work concerns Mammal ID only. Its $225$-class universe (\Cref{sec:target_class_definition}) is the candidate replacement for that mode's current class list.

\subsection{Preliminary Resources}\label{sec:pre_work}

Several resources and constraints were fixed before this work's own experimental work began. They are recorded here because they bound the design space the later chapters operate in.

\paragraph{The Production Baseline}
Mammal ID currently runs a two-stage pipeline: a \textit{YOLOv5s} detector fine-tuned to localize animals, followed by the \textit{SpeciesNet} classifier applied to each detected crop \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024a}. The full \textit{MegaDetector} \cite{beeryEfficientPipelineCamera2019} that normally feeds \textit{SpeciesNet} was too large for the device, so the smaller detector took its place. Two shortcomings motivated the present work. Its accuracy on the fine-grained mammal classes was to be improved, and both stages contribute errors to the final species label. Its latency is the sum of one detector pass and one classifier pass per detected animal, which strains the per-frame budget of the \textit{QCS605}. A third point constrains the replacement rather than motivating it. Swarovski Optik holds a commercial license for \textit{YOLOv5}, which is why that detector was adopted for mammal recognition and why any replacement is drawn from the same family (\Cref{sec:lit_yolo_family}). A single nano-scale detector that locates and classifies in one pass addresses both shortcomings, provided it matches the ensemble's accuracy. The production pipeline is therefore both the accuracy reference and the source of the student architecture family (\Cref{sec:lit_yolo_vs_ensemble,sec:model_selection}).

\paragraph{Class Universe}
The class universe was inherited rather than designed. It originates from the published taxonomy of \textit{SpeciesNet}, restricted to non-bird mammals and then filtered down by the AX Visio product owner to the labels that a binocular user can plausibly observe (\Cref{sec:target_class_definition}). Decisions about which species to include were based on observation counts scraped from \textit{GBIF} \cite{GBIF}. The premise was that species with very few recorded observations are both hard to train on and unlikely to be encountered.

\paragraph{Image Sources Assessed in Advance}
Candidate corpora were surveyed before sourcing began. The \textit{iNaturalist} competition datasets \cite{hornINaturalistSpeciesClassification2018} were preferred as an open collection of daylight photographs with heavy overlap onto the target class list. Camera-trap archives were examined and largely set aside, since their imagery is shaped by a fixed trap rather than by a handheld optic (\Cref{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy}).

\section{Structure}\label{sec:structure}

The remainder of this thesis is organized into four chapters and an appendix. The \hyperref[chapter:literature_review]{\textit{Literature Review}} traces object detection from its two architectural lineages to the nano-scale variants relevant here. It then covers generative image models, the sim-to-real gap, synthetic image quality evaluation, and the long-tail literature. Finally, it closes with knowledge distillation, embedded inference feasibility, and the evaluation literature that this work's metrics draw on. \hyperref[chapter:methods_implementation]{\textit{Methodology}} describes how the dataset and experiments were developed, including class taxonomy, image sourcing, curation, synthetic supplementation, and augmentation. It also covers a sub-study that compares ten generators across three prompt regimes and the training setup and evaluation framework.

The \hyperref[chapter:results]{\textit{Results}} chapter presents a comparison of generators, detection performance across training bands and test domains, a comparison of distillation versus direct fine-tuning, and a runtime benchmark on the proxy device. The \hyperref[chapter:discussion_conclusion]{\textit{Discussion}} chapter uses this evidence to answer the four research questions, state the limitations, and propose further work. The \hyperref[appendix]{\textit{Appendix}} holds supplementary material kept out of the main chapters, including the per-class band assignment, the frozen look-alike group listing, the full result tables, and qualitative sample galleries.
