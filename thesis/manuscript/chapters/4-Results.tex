\chapter{Results}\label{chapter:results}

This chapter is weighted to match where the experimental work actually went, on the same principle as \Cref{chapter:literature_review}, rather than giving each part of the original plan equal space.

Two results carry it. \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability} reports the practicability of synthetic training data through a controlled comparison of twelve generator and prompt-regime cells, which is the largest single experiment in the work. \Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison} reports detection accuracy across the four training bands and the three test domains defined in \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}, for the fine-tuned teacher model and for both student architectures. Those two sections hold the bulk of the measured evidence, and three findings in them are what the rest of the work argues from. Prompt detail matters more than generator identity. Synthetic imagery substitutes for half of a small real training set but not for all of it. And species classification rather than localization is where every model loses its accuracy.

\Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded} is deliberately much smaller. It reports one comparison between distillation and direct fine-tuning at a single hyperparameter point, and one runtime measurement on one proxy device with no optimization applied. Neither is the research program the work's central question calls for, and that section opens by saying so and by naming the time budget as the reason. Its two findings are nonetheless unambiguous within their scope: direct fine-tuning beats distillation on every axis measured, and no full-precision model reaches the project's latency target on target-adjacent hardware.

\Cref{sec:results_general_observations} closes with the observations that cross section boundaries and with the five scope boundaries that bound every number here, including the one evaluation rule this chapter deviated from.

\section{Practicability of Synthetic Training Data: Generator Comparison}\label{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability}

This section reports the controlled comparison designed in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison}: twelve generator$\times$prompt-regime cells, each supplying $1{,}200$ synthetic training images for the same $12$ classes to the same detector under the same schedule, each scored on the same $9{,}742$ real photographs. Two qualifications set from the outset determine how the numbers below should be read. The annotations underlying every cell are automatic rather than human-reviewed (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison_training}), so absolute accuracy is provisional even though the relative ranking is not obviously affected, every cell having been annotated identically. And of the three evaluation axes designed, only downstream utility was executed in full. The qualitative rubric and three of the four automatic proxies were specified and not carried out (\Cref{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}). The results are therefore a single-axis comparison, reported as such.

These numbers support comparison between generators, which is what they were constructed for, and do not support any claim about achievable accuracy.

Compressing the prompt costs roughly half the downstream accuracy, and does so almost identically for both models despite their differing capability. The effect is larger than most of the model-to-model differences in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking}: it moves the top-ranked cell to fifth place and the fourth to eighth. Within-group confusion moves in step, rising from $0.253$ to $0.457$ and from $0.218$ to $0.405$ respectively, indicating that what the long prompt buys is specifically the diagnostic detail on which species discrimination depends. This is what one would expect, since the diagnostic features are among the first content the compression cascade discards.

Two consequences follow. For the interpretation of \Cref{tab:generator-ranking}, the gap between the API and local tiers is substantially, though not wholly, a prompt-capacity effect rather than a pure quality effect. The local tier's disadvantage is real but it is at least in part a disadvantage of its text encoders rather than of its image generation. For practice, the finding inverts the intuitive ordering of concerns: how much detail a generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen, at least across the range of models tested here. The ablation covers two models rather than all of them, and the local tier has no unabridged-prompt cell at all, so this remains a demonstrated effect on two models rather than an established general law.

\subsection{Cost Does Not Predict Quality}\label{sec:results_cost_vs_quality}

Within the local tier, generation cost and downstream quality are unrelated to the point of inversion. The most expensive cell to produce is HiDream-I1, at nearly $48$ GPU-hours for $1{,}200$ images. This ranks eleventh of twelve, while the cheapest by two orders of magnitude, at under a third of an hour, ranks twelfth. The best cost-to-quality trade-off in the grid is SD 3.5 Medium, statistically indistinguishable from SD 3.5 Large at less than half its generation time. Model scale, denoising-step count and generation time all fail to predict the outcome. What does appear to predict it is the model family, with the Stable Diffusion 3.5 architecture and training recipe leading regardless of which size is used.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/plots/model_comparison_maxlen_cost_vs_map.png}
\caption[Generation cost against downstream accuracy]{Generation cost against downstream accuracy for the six locally-run generators. The absence of any trend is the result: the most and least expensive cells are the two weakest.}
\label{fig:generator-cost-vs-map}
\end{figure}

The same pattern holds across the API tier and is sharper still, because there the cost is monetary and directly comparable. The two \texttt{gpt-image-2} quality tiers differ by a factor of $2.9$ in price ($\$10.11$ against $\$29.36$ for the same $1{,}200$ images) and are statistically tied in outcome: a gap of $0.008$ against a combined standard deviation of roughly $0.018$. Paying almost three times more for the higher quality tier bought nothing measurable. On the other vendor, the incumbent and its cheaper variant are likewise indistinguishable within their spreads. In neither family is the cheaper tier the worse one.

No single cost axis spans the whole table, and none is offered: local cells are paid for in GPU-hours and API cells in currency, and reducing both to one number would require an assumed price of compute that would carry the conclusion.

\subsection{Per-Class Structure: Where the Ranking Comes From}\label{sec:results_per_class_structure}

The aggregate figures in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking} are not evenly sourced across the twelve classes, and the imbalance is itself the most important result in this section. Essentially all of the discriminating signal comes from the three data-rich anchor classes and the zebras: American black bear alone ranges from $0.072$ for the weakest cell to $0.326$ for the strongest, a spread of $4.5$ times. The six rare classes behave in the opposite way. Five of them (aye-aye, kinkajou, ringtail, saiga and the pangolin family) stay below $0.05$ average precision for \emph{every} cell in the grid, regardless of how well that same cell performs on the anchors. Ringtail never exceeds $0.010$. Water deer is the sole exception, and even it peaks at $0.066$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/plots/model_comparison_full_per_class_heatmap.png}
\caption[Per-class average precision across all cells]{Per-class average precision for every cell. The contrast between the data-rich columns, which carry the ranking, and the rare-class columns, which are uniformly near zero, is the section's central observation.}
\label{fig:generator-per-class-heatmap}
\end{figure}

This is a resolution limit of the evaluation axis rather than a finding about the generators. With at most a few dozen to a few hundred real test images per rare class and a detector fine-tuned on $100$ synthetic images of it, the measurement has no power to separate a good generator from a poor one on exactly the classes for which synthetic data was introduced in the first place.

A single case makes the point concretely. FLUX.2-klein renders kinkajous with a heavily ringed tail and a spotted coat, closer to a genet or civet than to the real animal, which has a plain golden-brown unringed tail. The defect is consistent across its images of that class and is specific to that model, since SD 3.5 Large and HiDream-I1 both render the species correctly from the same prompt text. It is, in other words, an unambiguous and visually obvious species error. \Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples} shows the error directly, alongside a real photograph and the two generators that avoid it. Its effect on the measured accuracy for that class is nil: FLUX.2-klein scores $0.0064$ and SD 3.5 Large $0.0058$, indistinguishable, both buried in the noise floor. A generator that draws the wrong animal is not penalized by the only axis this study executed. That is the strongest available argument for the qualitative rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation}, and the clearest cost of its not having been carried out.

Within-group confusion over the zebras tracks overall detector quality at both extremes but not through the middle of the ranking. The three lowest confusion rates ($0.218$ to $0.253$) belong to three of the four top-ranked cells, and the two highest ($0.628$ and $0.683$) to the two lowest-ranked. In between the ordering breaks down, and the incumbent is a visible exception, ranking third on accuracy while carrying a confusion rate ($0.339$) worse than that of two local cells. The reading is that species confusion among look-alikes is largely downstream of general detection quality rather than an independent failure mode that some generators exhibit and others avoid. This also means that a cell's confusion rate adds little information beyond its accuracy, and cannot stand in for the fine-grained qualitative judgment it might appear to substitute for.

\subsection{The Qualitative Rubric: Designed, Not Executed}\label{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}

The blind multi-rater rubric specified in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} was not carried out, nor were three of the four automatic proxies: teacher recognition confidence, per-class distributional distances, and prompt-adherence scoring. Only the automatic annotation yield was computed, and it discriminates weakly: the local cells fall between $93.6\%$ and $98.8\%$ single-detection yield, a range too narrow to separate them.

The omission is recorded here rather than left implicit because it bounds the section's conclusions in a specific and predictable way. The rubric was designed as the instrument for precisely the regime where downstream accuracy has no resolution, namely the rare classes. Its absence is why \Cref{sec:results_per_class_structure} can identify a visually obvious species error, shown directly in \Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples}, and simultaneously show that no executed measure detects it. The time budget is the reason, and the axis remains fully specified and directly executable on the archived images, which makes it a natural first item of further work (\Cref{sec:further_work}) rather than a gap requiring redesign.

\subsection{Practicability Verdict}\label{sec:results_practicability_verdict}

Three conclusions are supported by the evidence assembled here, and one is not.

First, on the question the sub-study exists to answer: the production choice of generator is not vindicated, but neither is it refuted. The incumbent ranks third of twelve, is beaten by roughly $28\%$ by \texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest tier under an identical prompt regime, and is statistically tied with the cheaper variant from its own vendor. It is a reasonable choice that was not the best available one, and the margin by which it was beaten is smaller than the margin attributable to prompt length. The original basis for that choice (that its output looked more realistic) turns out to have been a poor predictor: the models that look best are not reliably the models whose data trains the best detector, which is exactly the objection that prompted this sub-study.

Second, prompt detail matters more than generator identity across the range tested. Third, cheaper tiers are not worse tiers, in either vendor family or in the local grid, so generation cost is not usable as a proxy for training-data quality.

What is not supported is any conclusion about the rare, long-tail classes. This is to say, about the case that motivated synthetic data in this work at all. On those classes every generator is indistinguishable from every other under the only axis executed, and at least one generator is demonstrably drawing the wrong animal without penalty. The honest summary is that the comparison establishes a ranking for data-rich classes and establishes that the current instrument cannot rank generators for data-poor ones.

\paragraph{Limitations}
Beyond the deferred evaluation axes, six qualifications apply. The annotations are automatic and un-reviewed throughout. Seed counts are uneven: three per API cell but only two per local cell, short of the three the protocol itself recommends. The design is not a full factorial: the local tier has no unabridged-prompt cell and the API tier no capacity-matched one, so the prompt-length effect is demonstrated on two models rather than across the grid. Evaluation is real-only, with no mixed condition defined for this sub-study. One planned cell, the quality ceiling from the more expensive tier of the incumbent's vendor, was never generated, so the grid has no upper anchor. And three fairness asymmetries remain, disclosed in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_prompts}: the unabridged prompt was trimmed for one vendor's character cap on long-article species, negative prompts were unavailable to one local cell, and the two prompt regimes are not seed-matched to each other.

\paragraph{A Note on Seeds}
One episode during this study is worth reporting because it bears on how the table above should be read. At a single training seed, the incumbent scored $0.064$ and its cheaper variant $0.094$, and this was initially written up as a clear result: the lighter model beating the model it is a reduced version of. Adding a second seed moved the incumbent to $0.090$ and the conclusion to \enquote{indistinguishable}. Adding a third moved it to $0.105$ and reversed the direction of the gap outright. The final figure of $0.105 \pm 0.036$ has a spread roughly six times wider than any other API cell's, which after three seeds looks like a genuine property of that cell rather than an artifact of too few runs. The lesson is not merely that more seeds are better but that a single-seed difference of this size in this regime carries no information at all. Moreover, reporting a standard deviation of zero for a one-seed cell, as a naive aggregation does, actively conceals the problem.

Four models are reported. These are the \textit{MegaDetector} and \textit{SpeciesNet} ensemble off the shelf, the same ensemble with its classifier fine-tuned (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}), \textit{YOLOv5s}, and \textit{YOLO26n} trained by direct fine-tuning. Each model contributes one training run rather than a repeated trial, so no figure here carries a variance estimate. And the bands hold different species, assigned by data availability rather than at random (\Cref{sec:band_structure}), so any cross-band difference is confounded with how hard the species in each band happen to be.

One further property bounds the teacher's figures specifically, and it is recorded once here rather than beside each table. The ground-truth boxes of both test domains were produced by \textit{MegaDetector} (\Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}), and the teacher ensemble localizes with that same detector. Its localization is therefore scored partly against its own output. The visible signature is that its mAP averaged over intersection-over-union thresholds from $0.5$ to $0.95$ equals its mAP50 to three decimal places, which no single-stage detector reported here comes close to. Every teacher figure below is an upper bound for that reason, and the measured gap between the teacher and the students is an upper bound with it.

And average recall at $100$ detections reaches $0.845$ on the mixed test set against a mAP of $0.599$, so the limiting factor is how correct detections rank against false positives rather than a failure to recover the animal. \Cref{sec:results_granularity_gaps} decomposes where that loss goes.

The real-only breakout falls below the mixed headline for every model, by between $0.042$ and $0.089$ mAP. The ordering of the four models is nearly, but not entirely, preserved across the two domains. On the mixed test set \textit{YOLOv5s} at $0.496$ edges the off-the-shelf ensemble at $0.487$, and on the real test set that pair reverses to $0.407$ against $0.445$. Every other pairwise ordering holds on both domains. The choice of headline domain therefore moves exactly one adjacent pair in this table, which is worth knowing before \Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} asks whether the mixed default was the right default.

Two sensitivity checks leave the primary detector's headline essentially where it stands. Dropping the $9$ classes holding fewer than $30$ real test images moves the mixed figure from $0.599$ to $0.594$. Weighting classes by test-image count instead of averaging them equally gives $0.597$. The headline is therefore not an artifact of a handful of thin classes, which is the specific risk \Cref{sec:metric_selection} flagged.

One comparison \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} cannot make is the one that would anchor every other row in it. A zero-shot baseline exists for the teacher and not for the student, because the student's evaluation from raw COCO weights was never completed. The improvement attributable to fine-tuning \textit{YOLO26n} on this domain is therefore not measured anywhere in this work, only its absolute level. \Cref{sec:results_general_observations} records that gap again rather than leaving it implicit here.

On comparability outside this work, one figure qualifies and the rest do not. The real-only $\mathrm{mAP}_{\text{detect}}$ of $0.782$ for the primary detector is the only number here on the same footing as a published generic-detector result, for the reasons \Cref{sec:mixed_default} gives. The $225$-class fine-grained figures are not comparable in absolute terms to a published $80$-class COCO mAP, and a lower value is expected rather than evidence of a weaker detector.

Cross-group misclassification, duplicate detections and background false positives therefore all land in $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ without being separated. The figure is an upper bound on cross-group species confusion and not a measurement of it, and that distinction is the reason the decomposition is reported here as a diagnostic rather than as a finding about taxonomy.

The delta is negative almost everywhere. For the primary detector it is negative for $208$ of the $224$ banded classes, and for all $76$ classes in Band A and Band B without exception. Part of that gap is not domain shift at all, and separating the two parts is not possible from these artifacts. \paragraph{Watchdog verdict}
The trigger fired. \Cref{sec:mixed_default} states that a systematic discrepancy between the mixed and real results is the signal to revise the default evaluation axes, and the discrepancy in \Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta} is both large and systematic rather than marginal or mixed in sign. The synthetic component inflates the mixed headline relative to real-world performance for every model and every band, and it inflates it most for exactly the classes the mixed default was introduced to stabilize. The designed revision, which is to restore the real test set as the default and retain the mixed figure as secondary, was not carried out, because it would require rewriting every reported figure in this chapter within a time budget that did not allow it. That is a deviation from the stated protocol and is recorded as one.

Two things limit the damage, and neither repairs the deviation. Every headline figure in this chapter appears with its real-only breakout beside it, so no cross-model claim here rests on the mixed figure alone. And switching the default would change the cross-model ranking of \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} in one place only, where \textit{YOLOv5s} and the off-the-shelf ensemble exchange positions. The primary detector, the model choice, and the Band B against Band C conclusion of \Cref{sec:results_band_granularity} are all unaffected by which domain carries the headline. The honest summary is that the mixed default survived as a ranking instrument and failed as a description of real-world accuracy, and that \Cref{sec:further_work} inherits the revision.

A training-composition imbalance is the natural candidate explanation and is not verified here.

One check the grouping review asked for cannot be answered from these artifacts. The frozen table was to be validated by confirming that leopard and jaguar confuse heavily while lion and tiger do not, and the second half of that test needs cross-group confusions, which the evaluation suite emits only within groups. The rosette half is confirmed, with snow leopard, leopard and jaguar confusing each other $216$ times. The lion and tiger half remains unchecked. A block-diagonal confusion matrix over the groups was specified for this subsection and is not presented, since the pair counts above carry the same information at this level of concentration and no matrix artifact was generated.

\section{Knowledge Distillation and Embedded Feasibility}\label{sec:results_kd_and_embedded}

This section is much smaller than the question it addresses, and saying so first is more useful than discovering it halfway through. \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation,sec:lit_embedded_inference} survey a design space of distillation objectives, teacher and student pairings, temperature and weighting schedules, and quantization strategies for embedded targets. What was carried out against that space is one comparison between two training runs at one hyperparameter point, and one runtime measurement on one proxy device without any optimization applied. Neither is a sweep, and neither is the research program the work's central question calls for.

The reason is the time budget and nothing more principled than that. Roughly four fifths of the available time went into dataset construction, synthetic-image generation and the generator comparison of \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability}, and the distillation and deployment work ran on what was left. The consequence is recorded here as a scope decision rather than presented as the original plan, since the original plan was a temperature and weighting grid, a multi-animal subset cut, and a quantization-aware training pass. None of those ran. What follows is reported as indicative evidence at a single design point, and \Cref{sec:further_work} inherits the rest.

\subsection{Distillation vs.\ Direct Fine-Tuning: A Single Basic Comparison}\label{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}

Setup A and Setup B of \Cref{sec:augmentation_setups} are the two runs compared here, both training \textit{YOLO26n} for $225$ classes at $640$ px on the same data with the same schedule.

% for Setups A-C; sec:training_procedure supersedes that with YOLO26n, which is

Setup B takes the assigner's own targets untouched. The augmentation pipeline is the basic set of \Cref{sec:basic_augmentation} in both, shared through the same code path rather than reimplemented, so the intended single varied factor is the training signal. \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct} reports both.

Direct fine-tuning is the stronger recipe in every row of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, the confusion rate included, where the lower value is the better one. On the mixed test set it reaches $0.599$ against the distilled student's $0.560$, and on the real-only breakout $0.529$ against $0.479$. The result is a clean negative for the distillation hypothesis at this design point rather than an ambiguous one, and it holds on the case the hypothesis was expected to help most. Band A, whose training signal is entirely synthetic and thin, is where distillation loses by the widest margin of all, at $0.086$ mixed and $0.061$ real.

Neither run carries a confidence interval, so the size of the gap cannot be called significant. What can be said is that Setup B is ahead in all $17$ cells, across two test domains, three granularity levels, four bands and the validation metric the runs were selected on. A single seed can invert a difference of this magnitude, as \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows it doing in the generator comparison, but a direction that holds across seventeen partly independent views of one pair of runs is unlikely to be pure seed noise. The direction is treated as real and the magnitude as unquantified.

Three asymmetries between the two runs must be disclosed, because the first of them breaks the single-factor design \Cref{sec:augmentation_setups} specifies. The distilled run trained at an effective batch size of $16$ against $32$ for the direct run, both auto-reduced from a nominal $64$ by the memory probe of \Cref{sec:training_procedure}. The extra cached teacher vector per image is the plausible cause of the difference and was not separately confirmed.  % run logs: batch_size_effective=16 vs 32
Batch size is therefore a second varied factor and its contribution to the gap is unmeasured. Second, the distilled run ended at epoch $186$ of $200$ when an unhandled tracking-client exception followed a network outage on the training host, and its epoch-$185$ checkpoint is used as final. Its validation metric was still rising, from $0.724$ at epoch $175$ to $0.733$ at epoch $185$, so a resumed run would likely have gained a little more.  % docs/2026-09-07_model-comparison-teacher-and-students.md §3.3
Third, the direct run stopped on its own patience criterion at epoch $193$ with its best checkpoint at epoch $172$, so it was the more nearly converged of the two.

Four limits bound what this comparison establishes, and all four are scope boundaries rather than defects. It tests one $(T, \alpha)$ point, the default $T = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$, and not the grid \Cref{sec:kd_loss} was written around, so it rules out a benefit at that point and not distillation in general. It tests one teacher, the fine-tuned \textit{MegaDetector} and \textit{SpeciesNet} ensemble, at a parameter ratio of roughly $72$ to $1$, which \Cref{sec:kd_loss} already identifies as far outside the range the literature treats as safe and as a documented setting for negative transfer. It tests response-based distillation only, feature-based alignment having been excluded on architectural grounds before any run started. And the multi-animal subset cut, which is the concrete test of the supervision advantage distillation was expected to have, was never evaluated, so the one mechanism that might favor Setup A here remains unexamined.

\subsection{Runtime Benchmark on the Embedded Proxy}\label{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}

Every model evaluated in this work was exported and timed on a
\textit{Raspberry Pi 400}, the proxy whose relationship to the \textit{QCS605}
is set out in \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference}. No measurement was taken on
\textit{AX Visio} hardware, which was not available. The check is a feasibility
result for full-precision models on a target-adjacent CPU, not an optimization
result: no quantization, pruning or compression of any kind was applied.

The export path runs from each training checkpoint to \textit{TorchScript} and to \textit{ONNX}
at opset~$17$ with static batch-1 shapes. Because both detector pipelines
bypass \textit{Ultralytics}' high-level interface and store bare parameter
dictionaries, the exporter rebuilds each architecture through this project's own
model factories before tracing. Every exported graph was then checked against
its \textit{PyTorch} reference on the host before it reached the device, and all
five reproduce it to within floating-point accumulation noise.

\textit{MLPerf Tiny} convention discussed in \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference}:
$W_{\mathrm{infer}}$ covers the forward call alone, on an input tensor already

adds JPEG decode, letterboxing and post-processing, and is the figure that

No configuration measured here passes either. The fastest model, \textit{YOLO26n}, needs

Two observations concern the runtime rather than the models. ONNX Runtime was
faster than TorchScript for every model that ran under both, by factors of
$1.16$ to $1.75$. It was also the only runtime that executed every
configuration without aborting: multi-threaded TorchScript terminates with an
illegal-instruction fault on \textit{YOLO26n} on this ARMv8.0 core. Disabling
its oneDNN backend was tried as a remedy and extended that failure to every
model rather than removing it.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/torchscript_sigill_finding.md
Runtime selection on this class of hardware is therefore a correctness question
before it is a performance one. Second, no NCNN or GPU figure could be produced:
the device's Vulkan stack is functional, but the only available
ONNX-to-NCNN converter fails on both detectors, so the \textit{VideoCore VI} was
never reachable.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/ncnn_conversion_finding.md

Pre-processing is a small but non-trivial share of the per-frame cost, and it
scales with capture resolution rather than with model size. Decoding and
letterboxing a median test image costs $21$--$35$\,ms, while the same operation
on a native $4192\times3120$ \textit{AX Visio} still costs $256$--$275$\,ms. That
figure is comparable to a full \textit{YOLO26n} forward pass, and it is a cost
that a deployment reading frames at sensor resolution would pay on every frame.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/latency_summary.csv

The measurements are not thermally compromised. All $76$ cells were taken under
the \texttt{performance} governor with a monitor sampling core temperature,
frequency and the SoC throttle register throughout. None tripped the validity
gate, the peak temperature reached was $64.8$\,\textdegree C against an
$80$\,\textdegree C threshold, and the throttle register read zero for the
entire campaign. Sustained runs of several hundred iterations drifted by between
$+0.28\,\%$ and $+2.33\,\%$, which bounds the thermal effect on this passively
cooled device at a level well below the differences the table reports.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/thermal_summary.csv

Exporting the detectors and running them on a different instruction set does
not change what they compute. Over a fixed $600$-image subset, the device
reproduces between $98.97\,\%$ and $99.47\,\%$ of the host's detections at an
intersection-over-union of $0.99$ or better, and the subset mAP differs by at
most $0.0020$.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/parity_summary.json
That residual is best read against a same-machine control: host GPU and host
CPU, identical weights and identical code, already disagree on $0.15$--$0.31\,\%$
of detections, because floating-point accumulation order differs between
kernels and a near-tie in score can reorder which box survives. One of the six
comparisons falls marginally below the $99\,\%$ criterion set in advance, at
$98.97\,\%$. It is reported as a miss rather than absorbed by relaxing the
threshold after the fact, and its mAP delta stays inside tolerance.

The check also caught a defect that had nothing to do with hardware. An earlier
run reported a $47\,\%$ match with both device runtimes agreeing exactly with
each other. This pattern indicates differing weights, not differing
arithmetic. The direct fine-tuning checkpoint came from a training run still in
progress, and the file had been rewritten between the export and the generation
of the host reference. The export stage now freezes a hashed copy of each
source checkpoint, and the reference is built from that copy.

Three limits apply to everything above. The models are full-precision and
unoptimized, so the figures are an upper bound on latency rather than an
estimate of what the hardware can do. The campaign covers one device, and the
translation to the \textit{QCS605} rests on a published CPU-performance estimate
rather than a measurement of the target. Most importantly, the
\textit{QCS605}'s Hexagon~685 DSP has no analogue on the proxy, so the
accelerated path (the one the quantization literature in
\Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference} reports order-of-magnitude gains for, and the
one a shipping product would use) remains entirely unmeasured. Whether the
$30$\,ms budget is reachable on the target is therefore still an open question,
and \Cref{sec:further_work} treats it as the first item of further work.

\section{Cross-Cutting Observations}\label{sec:results_general_observations}

Three findings cut across the sections above and belong to none of them individually. This section states those, then fixes the scope boundaries that bound every number in the chapter, so that \Cref{chapter:discussion_conclusion} argues from a clear ledger rather than re-deriving one.

\paragraph{Training cost, reported but not comparable}
The four final runs occupied a single \textit{NVIDIA A40} for between roughly $21$ hours and $8.8$ days. The teacher's classifier fine-tune early-stopped at epoch $22$ after about $21$ hours. \textit{YOLOv5s} ran the full $200$ epochs in about $5.0$ days at batch size $32$. \textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning ran $193$ epochs in about $8.8$ days at batch size $32$, and the distilled run $186$ epochs in about $4.5$ days at batch size $16$.  % run directory logs, first and last epoch timestamps
Those wall-clock figures are not a throughput comparison and must not be read as one. Every one of the four runs overlapped in time with at least one other on the same host, so each was contending for the same device, and the per-run cost is confounded with what else was executing. The one cost figure that is clean is the validation pass, at roughly $12.5$ minutes per evaluation against roughly $50$ minutes for a training epoch (\Cref{sec:training_procedure}), which is why the evaluation interval became a tunable rather than a constant.

\paragraph{The two synthetic-data results agree}
\Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} concludes that the generator comparison establishes a ranking for data-rich classes and establishes that its instrument cannot rank generators for data-poor ones. \Cref{sec:results_band_granularity} reaches a compatible conclusion from a different direction. Synthetic imagery substitutes for half of a small real training set at little or no measurable cost, and it does not substitute for all of it. Band A loses roughly half the accuracy that the same budget of real images achieves on the real test set. Both results place the useful range of synthetic data in the middle of the availability spectrum and neither supports its use at the extreme of the long tail, which is the case that motivated generating it. The two sub-studies were designed independently and measure different things, so their agreement is corroboration rather than a shared assumption.

\Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} adds the mechanism that connects them. A model trained on synthetic images for a class scores far better on synthetic test images of that class than on real ones, by a median of $0.72$ mAP for Band A. The generator comparison could not see this because it evaluated on real photographs only. The band comparison could not see it in the mixed grid because Band A is partly scored on its own training domain there. Only the paired per-class delta exposes it, which is the argument for having kept that watchdog at all, even though the verdict it returned was that the default it guards should be revised.

\paragraph{The distillation and deployment results are indicative}
\Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded} rests on one comparison at one design point and one runtime measurement on one device, and both should be read as indicative rather than settled. The distillation result rules out a benefit at $T = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$ with this teacher and this student, and does not rule out a benefit elsewhere in the space \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation} surveys. The runtime result establishes that no full-precision model measured here reaches the project's latency target on a target-adjacent processor, and says nothing about the accelerated path a shipping product would use. \Cref{sec:further_work} treats both as first items rather than as closed questions.

\paragraph{Scope boundaries}
Five things this chapter does not report are stated here so that their absence is a boundary and not an omission discovered later. One further limit is a deviation rather than a boundary and is recorded where it arises, in the watchdog verdict of \Cref{sec:results_domain_shift}.

The \textbf{student zero-shot baseline} was never produced. An evaluation of \textit{YOLO26n} from raw COCO weights failed on a missing data directory and was not retried after that directory was fixed, so the chapter has no floor against which to measure what fine-tuning on this domain bought. Every student figure is an absolute level and not a gain.

The \textbf{distillation hyperparameter grid} was never swept. Only the default $(T, \alpha)$ point ran, so \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct} is one cell of the grid \Cref{sec:kd_loss} describes rather than a summary of it.

The \textbf{multi-animal subset} was never evaluated separately. That cut is the concrete test of the supervision advantage distillation was expected to hold over hard-label training, and it is also the setting the per-image teacher cache of \Cref{sec:kd_loss} approximates most crudely. Its absence means the one mechanism that could have favored distillation was not probed.

\textbf{Quantization-aware training} was deferred as a scope decision rather than attempted and abandoned. It is also the single largest remaining lever on the embedded target, since the accelerated path on the \textit{QCS605} is a quantized one and the full-precision figures of \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} miss the latency budget by roughly twelve times. Calling this chapter's deployment verdict final would require that pass.

The \textbf{blind human rating rubric} for the generator comparison was never run, as \Cref{sec:results_rubric_not_executed} records in full. It remains fully specified and directly executable on the archived images, and it is the axis that would resolve exactly the rare-class question the executed axis has no power over.
