\chapter{Results}\label{chapter:results}

This chapter reports the experiments in four sections. \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability} reports the practicability of synthetic training data through a controlled comparison of twelve generator and prompt-regime cells. \Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison} reports detection accuracy across the four training bands and the three test domains of \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}. \Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison} compares the distilled student with its directly fine-tuned counterpart at one design point. \Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark} measures the runtime of each model on the proxy device. Three findings from these sections recur throughout the remaining chapters: prompt detail matters more than generator identity, synthetic imagery substitutes for half of a small real training set but not for all of it, and species classification rather than localization is where every model loses accuracy.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Practicability of Synthetic Training Data: Generator Comparison}\label{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability}

This section reports the controlled comparison designed in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison}: twelve generator$\times$prompt-regime cells, each supplying $1{,}200$ synthetic training images for the same $12$ classes to the same detector under the same schedule, each scored on the same $9{,}742$ real photographs. Two qualifications determine how the numbers below should be read. Every cell was annotated automatically (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison_training}), so absolute accuracy is provisional even though the identical treatment leaves the ranking unaffected. And of the three evaluation axes designed, only downstream utility was executed (\Cref{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}).

\subsection{Prompt Length Versus Model Identity}\label{sec:results_prompt_length}

The controlled ablation of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_prompts} distinguishes between the two variables by varying the prompt length alone within a fixed model for two models with different levels of capability (\Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}).

Compressing the prompt costs roughly half the downstream accuracy, almost identically for both models despite their differing capabilities (\Cref{fig:prompt-length-ablation}). This effect is larger than most model-to-model differences in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking}. It moves the top-ranked cell to fifth place and the fourth cell to eighth place. Within-group confusion moves in step, from $0.253$ to $0.457$ and from $0.218$ to $0.405$, so the long prompt supplies the diagnostic detail on which species discrimination depends.

The gap between the API and local tiers is therefore substantially, though not entirely, a prompt-capacity effect. This makes the disadvantage of the local tier partly due to its text encoders. The practical ordering of concerns is inverted: how much detail a generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. The ablation study covers two models, and the local tier lacks an unabridged prompt cell. Thus, this is a demonstrated effect rather than a general law. Price is the remaining criterion before any detector is trained.

\subsection{Cost Does Not Predict Quality}\label{sec:results_cost_vs_quality}

Within the local tier, generation cost and downstream quality are unrelated to the point of inversion. HiDream-I1 is the most expensive cell to produce, at nearly $48$ GPU-hours for $1{,}200$ images. It ranks eleventh out of twelve, while the twelfth-ranked cell is the cheapest by two orders of magnitude. SD 3.5 Medium offers the best trade-off in the grid and is indistinguishable from SD 3.5 Large, despite having less than half the generation time. Model scale, denoising-step count and generation time all fail to predict the outcome. However, model family does predict the outcome, with the Stable Diffusion 3.5 architecture leading at either size.

\Cref{fig:generator-cost-vs-map} shows no trend within that tier. The API tier is even sharper because the cost is monetary. The two \texttt{gpt-image-2} quality tiers differ by a price factor of $2.9$ ($\$10.11$ against $\$29.36$ for $1{,}200$ images), and they are statistically tied: a gap of $0.008$ against a combined standard deviation of roughly $0.018$. Likewise, the production generator and its cheaper variant are indistinguishable within their spreads. Neither family's cheaper tier is worse, and no single cost axis spans the whole table. Reducing GPU-hours and currency to one number would require an assumed price of compute that would carry the conclusion.

\subsection{Per-Class Structure: Where the Ranking Comes From}\label{sec:results_per_class_structure}

The aggregate figures in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking} are not evenly sourced across the twelve classes, and that imbalance is this section's most important result. Nearly all of the discriminating signal comes from the three data-rich anchor classes and the zebras, with American black bear alone ranging from $0.072$ for the weakest cell to $0.326$ for the strongest. The six rare classes behave in the opposite way. Five of them, namely aye-aye, kinkajou, ringtail, saiga and the pangolin family, stay below an average precision of $0.05$ for \emph{every} cell in the grid. Water deer is the sole exception and peaks at $0.066$.

\Cref{fig:generator-per-class-heatmap} shows the contrast column by column. This is a resolution limit of the evaluation axis rather than an insight into the generators. With at most a few hundred real test images per rare class and a detector fine-tuned on $100$ synthetic images of it, the measurement cannot distinguish between generators in the exact classes for which synthetic data was introduced.

One case illustrates this point. FLUX.2-klein depicts kinkajous with heavily ringed tails and spotted coats, closer to a genet or civet than to the real animal, whose tail is plain golden-brown and unringed. This defect is consistent across its images of that class and specific to that model, since SD 3.5 Large and HiDream-I1 render the species correctly from the same prompt (\Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples}). Within-group confusion over the zebras tracks detector quality at the extremes and not through the middle of the ranking. The three lowest rates, ranging from $0.218$ to $0.253$, belong to three of the four top-ranked cells. Meanwhile, the two highest rates, $0.628$ and $0.683$, belong to the two lowest-ranked cells. Therefore, species confusion among look-alikes is largely downstream of general detection quality, so a cell's confusion rate cannot substitute for the qualitative judgment it appears to stand in for.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Execution Status of the Qualitative Rubric}\label{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}

The blind multi-rater rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} was not carried out, nor were three of the four automatic proxies. The one computed, automatic annotation yield, places the local cells between $93.6\%$ and $98.8\%$, too narrow a range to separate them. The rubric was the designed instrument for the setting in which downstream accuracy has no resolution, so its absence is why the kinkajou error is visible to the eye and to no executed measure.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Practicability Verdict}\label{sec:results_practicability_verdict}

Three conclusions are supported by the evidence assembled here, and one is not.

The choice of generator for production is neither vindicated nor refuted. The production generator ranks third out of twelve, falling roughly $28\%$ behind \texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest tier under an identical prompt regime and tying with the cheaper variant from its own vendor. This margin is smaller than the one attributable to prompt length. The original rationale for choosing this generator, that the output appeared more realistic, was an unreliable predictor. Second, prompt detail matters more than generator identity across the range tested. Third, cheaper tiers are not inferior in either vendor family or the local grid. Therefore, generation cost cannot be used as a proxy for training-data quality.

No conclusions can be drawn about the rare, long-tail classes, which is the case that motivated the use of synthetic data in this work at all. On those classes every generator is indistinguishable from every other under the only axis executed, and at least one is demonstrably drawing the wrong animal without penalty. The comparison establishes a ranking for data-rich classes and shows that the current instrument cannot rank generators for data-poor classes.

\paragraph{A note on seeds}
With a single training seed, the production generator scored $0.064$, while its cheaper variant scored $0.094$. A second seed increased the score to $0.090$, resulting in the conclusion that the two were \enquote{indistinguishable}, and a third seed increased the score to $0.105$, reversing the gap outright. A difference of this size in a single seed carries no information, and the same caution applies to the larger comparison that follows, in which every model contributes a single run.

\section{Detection Performance per Band on Each Test Domain}\label{sec:results_regime_comparison}

\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework} fixed one instrument and one reporting structure, and this section applies both to the trained detectors. Each figure below is from one evaluation-suite run against a fixed test set of $63{,}802$ real images and $11{,}250$ synthetic images. These images have $92{,}094$ and $11{,}264$ ground-truth boxes, respectively.
% scripts/training/yolo26n/model_exports/yolo26n-kd-bs16-20260916-101612/eval_run.log
The mixed test set is the union of the two sets, which are scored together as a single set of $75{,}052$ images. Four models are reported: the off-the-shelf \textit{MegaDetector} and \textit{SpeciesNet} ensemble, the same ensemble with a fine-tuned classifier (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}), \textit{YOLOv5s}, and \textit{YOLO26n} with direct fine-tuning. The distilled student is held back for \Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}.

Each fine-tuned model comes from a single training run on one \textit{NVIDIA A40}, lasting between roughly $21$ hours and $8.8$ days. The teacher's classifier fine-tune stopped early at epoch $22$ after about $21$ hours. \textit{YOLOv5s} ran $200$ epochs in about $5.0$ days, \textit{YOLO26n} ran $193$ epochs under direct fine-tuning in about $8.8$ days, and the distilled run finished $186$ epochs in about $4.5$ days.  % run directory logs, first and last epoch timestamps
These wall-clock figures are not a throughput comparison, because all four runs overlapped on the same host and contended for the same device.

Two qualifications apply to every number here. Each model contributes that single run, and the bootstrap confidence intervals of \Cref{sec:metric_selection} were never computed since the resample count remained at zero, so no difference between two cells is significant.
% evaluation_report.json: config.bootstrap_n = 0 in all five eval runs
The bands hold different species, which are assigned based on data availability rather than randomly (\Cref{sec:band_structure}). Therefore, every cross-band difference is confounded with how hard those species happen to be.

A third property specifically bounds the teacher's figures, and it is stated once here rather than beside each table. The ground-truth boxes of both test domains were produced by \textit{MegaDetector} (\Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}), and the teacher ensemble localizes with the same detector, so its localization is scored partly against its own output. The signature is that its mAP averaged over intersection-over-union thresholds from $0.5$ to $0.95$ equals its mAP50 to three decimal places, which no single-stage detector here approaches. Every teacher figure below is an upper bound, and the teacher-to-student gap with it.

The fine-tuned ensemble outperforms the off-the-shelf ensemble in both domains, achieving scores of $0.663$ and $0.599$ for mixed and real, respectively, compared to $0.487$ and $0.445$. The gain of $0.176$ for the mixed domain and $0.154$ for the real domain is the largest single effect in \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} and is subject to the circularity caveat in \Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}, since part of the fine-tuning corpus was admitted by agreeing with the pre-fine-tuning classifier. It is optimistic by an unknown and probably small amount.

Among the students, \textit{YOLO26n} is stronger in every category when fine-tuned directly and is smaller by a factor of $2.8$ in terms of parameter count. It beats \textit{YOLOv5s} by $0.103$ in mixed mAP and by $0.122$ in real-only breakout. A model that is simultaneously smaller and more accurate settles the architecture question on its own. \Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark} also measures it as the faster of the two. It is this work's primary detector for the rest of the chapter.

\Cref{tab:headline-coco12} fills the full twelve-statistic vector for that detector. Its small-object cells are empty because the minimum box-area rule applied during curation (\Cref{sec:quality_scoring_splits}) leaves no test instance below COCO's small-object threshold. And average recall at $100$ detections reaches $0.845$ mixed and $0.798$ real against a mAP of $0.599$ mixed and $0.529$ real, so the limiting factor is how correct detections rank against false positives rather than a failure to recover the animal.

The real-only breakout falls below the mixed headline for every model by between $0.042$ and $0.089$ mAP. The model ordering is nearly, but not entirely, preserved. On the mixed test set, \textit{YOLOv5s} edges the off-the-shelf ensemble with scores of $0.496$ and $0.487$, respectively. On the real test set, however, the scores reverse to $0.407$ and $0.445$, respectively. All other pairings remain consistent across both domains, indicating that the headline domain shifts by one adjacent pair. Two sensitivity checks confirm the primary detector's position: removing the $9$ classes with fewer than $30$ real test images yields a mixed figure of $0.594$, and weighting classes by test image count results in a figure of $0.597$.

\Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} cannot make one comparison: the one that would anchor every other row. There is a zero-shot baseline for the teacher, but not for the student because the student's evaluation using raw COCO weights failed due to a missing data directory and was never retried. Therefore, the gain that fine-tuning \textit{YOLO26n} on this domain produced is unmeasured, and every student figure in this work represents an absolute level rather than a gain over the initial level. The granularity ladder answers the question of where that level is lost.

The expected pattern does not appear. \Cref{sec:evaluation_axes} identifies a large $\Delta_{\text{fine}}$ alongside a small $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ as the look-alike signature and the most defensible failure mode for this dataset. The grouping itself bounds how large $\Delta_{\text{fine}}$ could ever have been. The frozen table of \Cref{sec:lookalike_groups} holds $174$ groups, of which only $30$ carry more than one member, covering $81$ of the $225$ classes.  % reports/lookalike_groups_v2.csv

\Cref{tab:granularity-gaps} cannot say what $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ consists of. The error-type decomposition, which was named the corroborating instrument in \Cref{sec:metric_selection}, was left as a future add-on.

Cross-group misclassifications, duplicate detections, and background false positives all end up in $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$, making it an upper bound on cross-group species confusion. This makes it a diagnostic rather than a finding about taxonomy. The next question is whether the training band moves it.

The central result is the contrast between Band B and Band C on the real test set, and this result is consistent in two of the three models. For the primary detector, half-synthetic training achieves a score of $0.588$, compared to an all-real training score of $0.591$ with the same budget. For the fine-tuned ensemble, the respective scores are $0.638$ and $0.639$. \textit{YOLOv5s} is the exception, with a score of $0.341$ versus $0.386$ and a preference for real photographs by $0.045$. At \texttt{coarse} granularity, however, the contrast reverses, with Band B surpassing Band C by $0.019$ for the primary detector and by $0.034$ for the ensemble. Replacing half of a small real training set with synthetic images changes accuracy by no more than $0.003$ mAP in two of the three models and by $0.045$ in the third. This is the strongest positive result that this work has regarding synthetic training data, and it is based on three single runs.

That conclusion does not extend to Band A. Training a class entirely on synthetic imagery leaves the primary detector at $0.300$ on the real test set, roughly half the $0.591$ the same budget of real photographs achieves, and \textit{YOLOv5s} at $0.058$. The direction is unambiguous across all three models even without confidence intervals, because the gap is an order of magnitude larger than the Band B against Band C difference beside it. Synthetic imagery substitutes for half a training set and not for all of it. Whether the failure is one of image realism or of pose and background diversity is not separable from these numbers.

The generator comparison of \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} reaches the same boundary from the other side. It ranks generators on the data-rich classes but cannot rank them on the data-poor classes for which synthetic data was generated in the first place. Both studies therefore place the useful range of synthetic imagery in the middle of the availability spectrum, where it supplements a small real training set. Neither study supports it at the long-tail end that motivated generating it. The two studies were designed independently, so the shared boundary does not follow from a shared assumption.

The same grid on the mixed test set shows smaller gaps, which argues for reporting both. Band A increases from $0.300$ to $0.579$ for the primary detector when its training domain is included in the test set. Meanwhile, Band D decreases from $0.742$ real to $0.726$ mixed.

Two smaller observations remain. First, the \texttt{coarse} column falls below the \texttt{fine} column in several cells. This is most visible in Band C for the primary detector, where it is $0.597$ against $0.614$ on the mixed test set. This is the duplicate-merge penalty that \Cref{sec:evaluation_axes} calls the conservative direction. Additionally, fine-tuning the teacher's classifier lowers Band A's accuracy on real photographs rather than raising it, from $0.324$ off the shelf to $0.182$ fine-tuned.  % model_exports/pretrained/eval/eval_band_grid.csv
Adapting it to $225$ wildlife classes loses generic recognition capability on the classes whose supervision is entirely synthetic, a loss the aggregate of \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} hides.

\paragraph{Consequence for the mixed default}
\Cref{sec:mixed_default} states that a systematic discrepancy between the mixed and real results requires revising the default evaluation axes. The discrepancy in \Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta} is large and one-directional rather than marginal or mixed in sign. The synthetic component inflates the mixed headline for every model and every band, most of all in exactly the classes the mixed default was introduced to stabilize. The revision, which would restore the real test set as the default while keeping the mixed figure as secondary, was not carried out. It would have required rewriting every reported figure in this chapter, which was not possible within the allotted time. This deviation from the stated protocol is recorded as such.

Two reporting rules mitigate the damage, but do not repair it. Each headline figure in this chapter appears with its real-only breakout beside it, so no cross-model claim rests on the mixed figure alone. Switching the default would only change the ranking of \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model}, where \textit{YOLOv5s} and the off-the-shelf ensemble would exchange positions. The mixed default holds as a ranking instrument but not as a description of accuracy on real photographs. The revision is carried as further work (\Cref{sec:further_work}).

Confusion within look-alike groups is common and unevenly distributed. A training-composition imbalance is the natural candidate and is not verified here.

One question from the grouping review cannot be answered using these artifacts. Confirming that lion and tiger are not confused with each other requires cross-group counts, which the suite only provides within groups. The rosette half is confirmed with $216$ mutual confusions among the snow leopard, leopard, and jaguar. All of this concerns the student trained by direct fine-tuning, which leaves its training recipe and deployment cost untested.

Direct fine-tuning is the stronger recipe in every row of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, the confusion rate included, where the lower value is better. On the mixed test set, direct fine-tuning reaches $0.599$, compared to the distilled student's $0.560$. On the real-only breakout, direct fine-tuning reaches $0.529$, compared to the distilled student's $0.479$. The negative is clean rather than ambiguous, and it holds on the case the hypothesis was expected to help most: Band A, whose training signal is entirely synthetic and thin, is where distillation loses by the widest margin, at $0.086$ mixed and $0.061$ real. However, neither run carries a confidence interval, so the gap cannot be considered significant. Nevertheless, Setup B leads in all $17$ cells across two test domains, three granularity levels, four bands, and the validation metric on which the runs were selected. A single seed can invert a difference of this magnitude, as \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} demonstrates, so the direction is considered real, and the magnitude is considered unquantified.

Three asymmetries must be disclosed because the first one breaks the single-factor design that \Cref{sec:augmentation_setups} specifies. The distilled run was trained at an effective batch size of $16$, whereas the direct run was trained at $32$. Both were auto-reduced from a nominal $64$ by the memory probe of \Cref{sec:training_procedure}. The extra cached teacher vector per image is the plausible and unconfirmed cause.  % run logs: batch_size_effective=16 vs 32
Batch size is therefore a second varied factor whose contribution to the gap is unmeasured. Second, the distilled run ended at epoch $186$ out of $200$ due to an unhandled tracking-client exception. Its epoch $185$ checkpoint was used as the final one, even though the validation metric was still rising from $0.724$ at epoch $175$ to $0.733$ at epoch $185$.  % docs/2026-09-07_model-comparison-teacher-and-students.md 3.3
Third, the direct run stopped on its own patience criterion at epoch $193$ with its best checkpoint at epoch $172$, so it was the more nearly converged of the two.

Four limits bound what the comparison establishes, and all four are scope boundaries rather than defects. The test is performed on one $(T, \alpha)$ point, not the \Cref{sec:kd_loss} grid, so a benefit is ruled out at that point, not distillation in general. It tests one teacher, the fine-tuned ensemble, at a parameter ratio of about $70$ to $1$. \Cref{sec:kd_loss} identifies this ratio as being far outside the range that the literature considers safe. It is also a documented setting for negative transfer. It tests response-based distillation only, feature-based alignment having been excluded on architectural grounds before any run started. The multi-animal subset cut, which was expected to demonstrate the supervision advantage of distillation, was never evaluated. Thus, the one mechanism that might favor Setup A remains unexamined. Regardless of which recipe wins on accuracy, the student still has to operate within a per-frame budget.

\section{Runtime Benchmark on the Proxy Device}\label{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}

Every model was exported and timed on a \textit{Raspberry Pi 400} rather than on the \textit{QCS605}, because the target's Hexagon 685 DSP is tied to a 2018-era vendor stack whose inconsistently documented operator support makes routine model iteration a driver-debugging exercise. The board's four Cortex-A72 cores are estimated to run $10$ to $15\%$ slower than the target's Kryo 300 cores, so the proxy supplies a conservative floor rather than a ceiling to discount.  % docs/2026-03-09_hardware-proxy-selection.md
There are two limits to that inference. First, the board has no analogue to the Hexagon 685 DSP. Second, its Cortex-A72 predates the ARMv8.2 extensions, so it provides neither the $8$-bit dot-product instruction nor the half-precision arithmetic that the Kryo 300 cores do.  % reports/embedded_benchmark/device_profile.json
No measurements were taken on the \textit{AX Visio} hardware, as it was unavailable. The result is a feasibility check for full-precision models on a CPU adjacent to the target, with no quantization, pruning, or compression applied.

Each checkpoint was exported to \textit{TorchScript} and to \textit{ONNX} at static batch-1 shapes, and every graph reproduced its \textit{PyTorch} reference on the host to within floating-point accumulation noise (\Cref{sec:appendix_embedded_export}).
