\chapter{Results}\label{chapter:results}

This chapter reports two experiments. \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability} reports the practicability of synthetic training data through a controlled comparison of twelve generator and prompt-regime cells. \Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison} reports detection accuracy across the four training bands and the three test domains of \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}. Three findings from them recur throughout the remaining chapters: prompt detail matters more than generator identity, synthetic imagery substitutes for half of a small real training set but not for all of it, and species classification rather than localization is where every model loses its accuracy. \Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded} reports one distillation comparison and one runtime measurement, and \Cref{sec:results_general_observations} collects what crosses section boundaries.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Practicability of Synthetic Training Data: Generator Comparison}\label{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability}

This section reports the controlled comparison designed in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison}: twelve generator$\times$prompt-regime cells, each supplying $1{,}200$ synthetic training images for the same $12$ classes to the same detector under the same schedule, each scored on the same $9{,}742$ real photographs. Two qualifications determine how the numbers below should be read. Every cell was annotated automatically (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison_training}), so absolute accuracy is provisional even though the identical treatment leaves the ranking unaffected. And of the three evaluation axes designed, only downstream utility was executed (\Cref{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}).

\subsection{Prompt Length Versus Model Identity}\label{sec:results_prompt_length}

The controlled ablation of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_prompts} separates those two variables by varying prompt length alone, within a fixed model, for two models at different capability levels (\Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}).

Compressing the prompt costs roughly half the downstream accuracy, almost identically for both models despite their differing capability (\Cref{fig:prompt-length-ablation}). The effect is larger than most model-to-model differences in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking}, moving the top-ranked cell to fifth place and the fourth to eighth. Within-group confusion moves in step, from $0.253$ to $0.457$ and from $0.218$ to $0.405$, so the long prompt supplies the diagnostic detail on which species discrimination depends.

The gap between the API and local tiers is therefore substantially, though not wholly, a prompt-capacity effect, which makes the local tier's disadvantage partly one of its text encoders. The practical ordering of concerns inverts: how much detail a generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. The ablation covers two models and the local tier has no unabridged-prompt cell, so this is a demonstrated effect rather than a general law. The remaining criterion available before any detector is trained is price.

\subsection{Cost Does Not Predict Quality}\label{sec:results_cost_vs_quality}

Within the local tier, generation cost and downstream quality are unrelated to the point of inversion. The most expensive cell to produce is HiDream-I1, at nearly $48$ GPU-hours for $1{,}200$ images, and it ranks eleventh of twelve, while the cheapest by two orders of magnitude ranks twelfth. SD 3.5 Medium is the best trade-off in the grid, indistinguishable from SD 3.5 Large at less than half its generation time. Model scale, denoising-step count and generation time all fail to predict the outcome. Model family does predict it, with the Stable Diffusion 3.5 architecture leading at either size.

\Cref{fig:generator-cost-vs-map} shows no trend across that tier. The API tier is sharper still, because there the cost is monetary. The two \texttt{gpt-image-2} quality tiers differ by a factor of $2.9$ in price ($\$10.11$ against $\$29.36$ for the same $1{,}200$ images) and are statistically tied, a gap of $0.008$ against a combined standard deviation of roughly $0.018$. The incumbent and its cheaper variant are likewise indistinguishable within their spreads. In neither family is the cheaper tier the worse one, and no single cost axis spans the whole table, since reducing GPU-hours and currency to one number would require an assumed price of compute that would carry the conclusion.

\subsection{Per-Class Structure: Where the Ranking Comes From}\label{sec:results_per_class_structure}

The aggregate figures in \Cref{tab:generator-ranking} are not evenly sourced across the twelve classes, and that imbalance is this section's most important result. Essentially all of the discriminating signal comes from the three data-rich anchor classes and the zebras, with American black bear alone ranging from $0.072$ for the weakest cell to $0.326$ for the strongest. The six rare classes behave in the opposite way. Five of them, namely aye-aye, kinkajou, ringtail, saiga and the pangolin family, stay below $0.05$ average precision for \emph{every} cell in the grid. Water deer is the sole exception and peaks at $0.066$.

\centering

\caption[Per-class average precision across all cells]{Per-class average precision for every cell. The contrast between the data-rich columns, which carry the ranking, and the near-zero rare-class columns is the section's central observation.}

\Cref{fig:generator-per-class-heatmap} shows that contrast column by column. It is a resolution limit of the evaluation axis rather than a finding about the generators: with at most a few hundred real test images per rare class and a detector fine-tuned on $100$ synthetic images of it, the measurement cannot separate generators on exactly the classes synthetic data was introduced for.

One case makes the point concretely. FLUX.2-klein renders kinkajous with a heavily ringed tail and a spotted coat, closer to a genet or civet than to the real animal, whose tail is plain golden-brown and unringed. The defect is consistent across its images of that class and specific to that model, since SD 3.5 Large and HiDream-I1 render the species correctly from the same prompt (\Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples}). Its effect on measured accuracy is nil, at $0.0064$ against SD 3.5 Large's $0.0058$, so a generator that draws the wrong animal goes unpenalized by the only axis this study executed.

Within-group confusion over the zebras tracks detector quality at the extremes and not through the middle of the ranking. Species confusion among look-alikes is therefore largely downstream of general detection quality, so a cell's confusion rate cannot substitute for the qualitative judgment it appears to stand in for.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Execution Status of the Qualitative Rubric}\label{sec:results_rubric_not_executed}

The blind multi-rater rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} was not carried out, nor were three of the four automatic proxies. The one computed, automatic annotation yield, places the local cells between $93.6\%$ and $98.8\%$, too narrow a range to separate them. The rubric was the designed instrument for the setting in which downstream accuracy has no resolution, so its absence is why the kinkajou error is visible to the eye and to no executed measure.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Practicability Verdict}\label{sec:results_practicability_verdict}

Three conclusions are supported by the evidence assembled here, and one is not.

The production choice of generator is not vindicated and not refuted. The incumbent ranks third of twelve, is beaten by roughly $28\%$ by \texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest tier under an identical prompt regime, and is tied with the cheaper variant from its own vendor. That margin is smaller than the one attributable to prompt length, and the original basis for the choice, that the output looked more realistic, was a poor predictor. Second, prompt detail matters more than generator identity across the range tested. Third, cheaper tiers are not worse tiers in either vendor family or in the local grid, so generation cost is not usable as a proxy for training-data quality.

What is not supported is any conclusion about the rare, long-tail classes, which is the case that motivated synthetic data in this work at all. On those classes every generator is indistinguishable from every other under the only axis executed, and at least one is demonstrably drawing the wrong animal without penalty. The comparison establishes a ranking for data-rich classes and establishes that the current instrument cannot rank generators for data-poor ones.

\paragraph{A note on seeds}
At a single training seed the incumbent scored $0.064$ against its cheaper variant's $0.094$. A second seed moved it to $0.090$ and the conclusion to \enquote{indistinguishable}, and a third moved it to $0.105$ and reversed the gap outright. A single-seed difference of this size carries no information, and the same caution governs the larger comparison that follows, where every model contributes a single run.

\section{Detection Performance Across Training Bands and Test Domains}\label{sec:results_regime_comparison}

\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework} fixed one instrument and one reporting structure, and this section applies both to the trained detectors. Every figure below comes from one evaluation-suite run against a fixed test set of $63{,}802$ real and $11{,}250$ synthetic images, carrying $92{,}094$ and $11{,}264$ ground-truth boxes.
% scripts/training/yolo26n/model_exports/yolo26n-kd-bs16-20260916-101612/eval_run.log
The mixed test set is the union of the two, scored together as a single set of $75{,}052$ images. Four models are reported: the \textit{MegaDetector} and \textit{SpeciesNet} ensemble off the shelf, the same ensemble with its classifier fine-tuned (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}), \textit{YOLOv5s}, and \textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning. The distilled student is held back for \Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}.

Two qualifications apply to every number here. Each model contributes one training run, and the bootstrap confidence intervals of \Cref{sec:metric_selection} were never computed, since the resample count stayed at zero, so no difference between two cells is significant.
% evaluation_report.json: config.bootstrap_n = 0 in all five eval runs
And the bands hold different species, assigned by data availability rather than at random (\Cref{sec:band_structure}), so every cross-band difference is confounded with how hard those species happen to be.

A third property bounds the teacher's figures specifically, and it is stated once here rather than beside each table. The ground-truth boxes of both test domains were produced by \textit{MegaDetector} (\Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}), and the teacher ensemble localizes with that same detector, so its localization is scored partly against its own output. The signature is that its mAP averaged over intersection-over-union thresholds from $0.5$ to $0.95$ equals its mAP50 to three decimal places, which no single-stage detector here approaches. Every teacher figure below is an upper bound, and the teacher-to-student gap with it.

One comparison \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} cannot make is the one that would anchor every other row in it. A zero-shot baseline exists for the teacher and not for the student, because the student's evaluation from raw COCO weights failed on a missing data directory and was never retried. What fine-tuning \textit{YOLO26n} on this domain bought is therefore unmeasured, and every student figure in this work is an absolute level rather than a gain over the floor it started from. Where that level is lost is a question the granularity ladder answers.

The central result is the Band B against Band C contrast on the real test set, and it is consistent in two of the three models. For the primary detector, half-synthetic training reaches $0.588$ against $0.591$ for all-real training at the same budget, and for the fine-tuned ensemble the same pair reads $0.638$ against $0.639$. \textit{YOLOv5s} is the exception at $0.341$ against $0.386$, favoring real photographs by $0.045$. That is the strongest positive result this work holds about synthetic training data, and it rests on three single runs.

That conclusion does not extend to Band A. Training a class entirely on synthetic imagery leaves the primary detector at $0.300$ on the real test set, roughly half the $0.591$ the same budget of real photographs achieves, and \textit{YOLOv5s} at $0.058$. The direction is unambiguous across all three models even without confidence intervals, because the gap is an order of magnitude larger than the Band B against Band C difference beside it. Synthetic imagery substitutes for half a training set and not for all of it. Whether the failure is one of image realism or of pose and background diversity is not separable from these numbers.

The same grid on the mixed test set shows smaller gaps, which is itself an argument for reporting both. Band A rises from $0.300$ to $0.579$ for the primary detector once its own training domain is folded into the test set, while Band D moves the other way, from $0.742$ real to $0.726$ mixed.

Two smaller observations remain. The \texttt{coarse} column falls below the \texttt{fine} column in several cells, most visibly Band C for the primary detector at $0.597$ against $0.614$ on the mixed test set, which is the duplicate-merge penalty \Cref{sec:evaluation_axes} calls the conservative direction. And fine-tuning the teacher's classifier lowers Band A accuracy on real photographs rather than raising it, from $0.324$ off the shelf to $0.182$ fine-tuned.  % model_exports/pretrained/eval/eval_band_grid.csv
Adapting it to $225$ wildlife classes loses generic recognition capability on the classes whose supervision is entirely synthetic, a loss the aggregate of \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} hides. The size of the mixed-against-real discrepancy in this grid is what the standing watchdog was defined to test.

\paragraph{Watchdog verdict}
The trigger fired. \Cref{sec:mixed_default} states that a systematic discrepancy between the mixed and real results is the signal to revise the default evaluation axes, and the discrepancy in \Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta} is large and one-directional rather than marginal or mixed in sign. The synthetic component inflates the mixed headline for every model and every band, most for exactly the classes the mixed default was introduced to stabilize. The designed revision, which is to restore the real test set as the default and retain the mixed figure as secondary, was not carried out, because it would require rewriting every reported figure in this chapter within a time budget that did not allow it. That is a deviation from the stated protocol and is recorded as one.

Two reporting rules limit the damage without repairing it. Every headline figure in this chapter appears with its real-only breakout beside it, so no cross-model claim rests on the mixed figure alone. And switching the default would move the ranking of \Cref{tab:headline-cross-model} in one place only, where \textit{YOLOv5s} and the off-the-shelf ensemble exchange positions. The mixed default survived as a ranking instrument and failed as a description of real-world accuracy, and the revision is carried as further work (\Cref{sec:further_work}).

Test-set size does not explain the direction, since the Asian elephant carries $1{,}393$ real test images against the African elephant's $500$.  % evaluation_report.json tier3_per_class
A training-composition imbalance is the natural candidate and is not verified here.

One check the grouping review asked for cannot be answered from these artifacts: confirming that lion and tiger are not confused with each other needs cross-group counts, which the suite emits only within groups. The rosette half is confirmed at $216$ mutual confusions among snow leopard, leopard and jaguar. Everything to this point concerns the student trained by direct fine-tuning, which leaves its training recipe and its deployment cost untested.

Direct fine-tuning is the stronger recipe in every row of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, the confusion rate included, where the lower value is better. On the mixed test set it reaches $0.599$ against the distilled student's $0.560$, and on the real-only breakout $0.529$ against $0.479$. The negative is clean rather than ambiguous, and it holds on the case the hypothesis was expected to help most: Band A, whose training signal is entirely synthetic and thin, is where distillation loses by the widest margin, at $0.086$ mixed and $0.061$ real. Neither run carries a confidence interval, so the gap cannot be called significant. Setup B nonetheless leads in all $17$ cells, across two test domains, three granularity levels, four bands and the validation metric the runs were selected on. A single seed can invert a difference of this magnitude, as \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows it doing, so the direction is treated as real and the magnitude as unquantified.

Three asymmetries must be disclosed, because the first breaks the single-factor design \Cref{sec:augmentation_setups} specifies. The distilled run trained at an effective batch size of $16$ against $32$ for the direct run, both auto-reduced from a nominal $64$ by the memory probe of \Cref{sec:training_procedure}, the extra cached teacher vector per image being the plausible and unconfirmed cause.  % run logs: batch_size_effective=16 vs 32
Batch size is therefore a second varied factor whose contribution to the gap is unmeasured. Second, the distilled run ended at epoch $186$ of $200$ after an unhandled tracking-client exception, and its epoch-$185$ checkpoint is used as final with the validation metric still rising, from $0.724$ at epoch $175$ to $0.733$ at epoch $185$.  % docs/2026-09-07_model-comparison-teacher-and-students.md 3.3
Third, the direct run stopped on its own patience criterion at epoch $193$ with its best checkpoint at epoch $172$, so it was the more nearly converged of the two.

Four limits bound what the comparison establishes, and all four are scope boundaries rather than defects. It tests one $(T, \alpha)$ point and not the grid \Cref{sec:kd_loss} was written around, so it rules out a benefit at that point and not distillation in general. It tests one teacher, the fine-tuned ensemble, at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$, which \Cref{sec:kd_loss} identifies as far outside the range the literature treats as safe and a documented setting for negative transfer. It tests response-based distillation only, feature-based alignment having been excluded on architectural grounds before any run started. And the multi-animal subset cut, the concrete test of the supervision advantage distillation was expected to have, was never evaluated, so the one mechanism that might favor Setup A remains unexamined. Whichever recipe wins on accuracy, the student still has to run inside a per-frame budget.

The board's four Cortex-A72 cores are estimated to run $10$ to $15\%$ slower than the target's Kryo 300 cores, so the proxy supplies a conservative floor rather than a ceiling to discount.  % docs/2026-03-09_hardware-proxy-selection.md

\section{Cross-Cutting Observations}\label{sec:results_general_observations}

Two observations span the sections above and belong to none of them.

\paragraph{Training cost}
The four final runs occupied a single \textit{NVIDIA A40} for between roughly $21$ hours and $8.8$ days: the teacher's classifier fine-tune early-stopped at epoch $22$ after about $21$ hours, \textit{YOLOv5s} ran $200$ epochs in about $5.0$ days, \textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning $193$ epochs in about $8.8$ days, and the distilled run $186$ epochs in about $4.5$ days.  % run directory logs, first and last epoch timestamps
These wall-clock figures are not a throughput comparison, because all four runs overlapped on the same host and each was contending for the same device.

\paragraph{The two synthetic-data results agree}
\Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} concludes that the generator comparison ranks generators for data-rich classes and cannot rank them for data-poor ones. \Cref{sec:results_band_granularity} reaches a compatible conclusion from a different direction, since synthetic imagery substitutes for half of a small real training set at little or no measurable cost while Band A loses roughly half the accuracy the same budget of real photographs achieves. Both place the useful range of synthetic data in the middle of the availability spectrum, and neither supports its use at the extreme of the long tail that motivated generating it. The two sub-studies were designed independently, so their agreement is corroboration rather than a shared assumption.

\Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} supplies the mechanism, since a model trained on synthetic images for a class scores better on synthetic test images of it than on real ones by a median of $0.72$ mAP for Band A. Only the paired per-class delta exposes that, which is the argument for having kept the watchdog even though its verdict was that the default it guards should be revised.
