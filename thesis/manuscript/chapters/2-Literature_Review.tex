\section{Object Detection for Wildlife Monitoring}\label{sec:lit_object_detection}

Modern object detection descends from two lineages that emerged within a few years of each other: two-stage region-proposal detectors and single-stage detectors. The two-stage, region-proposal lineage first proposes candidate regions and then classifies and refines them, reaching its mature form once the proposal step itself became a learned, end-to-end-trainable network \cite{girshickRichFeatureHierarchies2014a,renFasterRCNNRealTime2017}. Later, a top-down, multi-scale feature hierarchy was added so that objects of widely differing apparent sizes could be detected by the feature level suited to them \cite{linFeaturePyramidNetworks2017}. This is common in wildlife imagery, where an animal may occupy a few pixels or most of the frame depending on its distance.

The single-stage lineage eliminated the separate proposal step. \textit{YOLO}, for \enquote{you only look once}, reframed detection as a single regression over the whole image \cite{redmonYouOnlyLook2016a}, and multi-scale default boxes narrowed the resulting accuracy gap while retaining one forward pass \cite{liuSSDSingleShot2016a}. Correcting the inherent foreground-background class imbalance of dense single-stage prediction closed most of what remained \cite{linFocalLossDense2017}.

Two-stage detectors keep a modest accuracy advantage, at the cost of two sequential network evaluations per image. In contrast, single-stage detectors sacrifice some accuracy for a fixed, single-pass cost that does not increase with the number of candidate objects in a scene. Since the target hardware must answer while the animal is still in view, only the single-stage lineage is considered further.

\subsection{Challenges in Wildlife Species Recognition}\label{sec:lit_wildlife_challenges}

In addition to the general architectural trajectory mentioned above, recognizing wildlife species poses three recurring challenges that influence the specific choices made elsewhere in this work.

First, a detector must answer two questions for every animal: where it is and what it is. General benchmarks make both questions difficult. In a binocular frame, the animal is usually the salient object, so answering the first question is comparatively easy. The second question, \textbf{species classification}, is where the difficulty lies (\Cref{sec:results_granularity_gaps}). Distinguishing wildlife species is fundamentally a fine-grained visual classification problem. Unlike COCO-style categories (car, dog, person), many pairs of species differ only in subtle, localized visual cues rather than in gross shape or context \cite{weiFineGrainedImageAnalysis2022}. In the target taxonomy of this work, this shows up as groups of near-identical species. The plains-zebra/Grévy's-zebra, Eurasian-lynx/bobcat, and small-gazelle groupings are instances of the intra-order visual homogeneity that fine-grained recognition research identifies as its core difficulty \cite{weiFineGrainedImageAnalysis2022}. These groupings were later formalized as confusion clusters in the evaluation methodology (\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}).

Second, ecological image collections are long-tailed by nature. A handful of common and easily photographed species dominate any naturally collected corpus, while most species are represented by comparatively few images \cite{cuiClassBalancedLossBased2019,zhangDeepLongTailedLearning2023}. The sourcing effort for this work encountered the same imbalance, species by species (\Cref{sec:corpus_composition}).

Third, and specific to the wildlife domain, models trained on one visual domain generalize poorly to another. Camera-trap classifiers and detectors that perform well at their training locations degrade sharply at new, unseen camera locations \cite{beeryRecognitionTerraIncognita2018}. Background, lighting, and camera geometry are confounded with location rather than with the animal itself, and this confound is what drives the degradation.

Larger deployed camera-trap classifiers reinforce the same pattern. Cross-project transfer between ecologically similar studies still costs measurable accuracy \cite{williIdentifyingAnimalSpecies2019}, and cross-continental transfer can cost considerably more: one large-scale camera-trap classifier's species-level accuracy fell from $98\%$ in-region to $82\%$ on an out-of-sample Canadian ungulate dataset \cite{tabakMachineLearningClassify2019}. That drop occurred despite the same underlying kind of network having demonstrated near-human accuracy on its own training distribution \cite{norouzzadehAutomaticallyIdentifyingCounting2018}.

This is precisely the domain gap that led to the rejection of the camera-trap archives of \textit{LILA BC}, a public repository of ecological data sets, as a source of training data for this work (\Cref{sec:sourcing_execution}). Camera traps are static, fixed-position sensors that are frequently infrared-triggered, whereas the target deployment is a handheld, daylight, close-to-mid-range binocular observation scenario. The two distributions differ in exactly the location-, lighting-, and viewpoint-confounded ways this line of work documents.

More broadly, the wildlife-monitoring machine-learning community has converged on the view that such domain shift, rather than raw model capacity, is now a primary obstacle to deploying automated recognition at scale \cite{tuiaPerspectivesMachineLearning2022}. Capacity nonetheless sets the outer bound on how many species one deployable detector can separate, which is what the single-stage family below has to supply.

\subsection{The YOLO Model Family}\label{sec:lit_yolo_family}

Within the single-stage lineage, the YOLO family evolved along two intertwined axes: from anchor-based to anchor-free box prediction, and from non-maximum-suppression (NMS)-dependent to increasingly NMS-free inference. First, anchor boxes were learned by clustering the training-set box distribution \cite{redmonYOLO9000BetterFaster2017}. Then, they were dropped entirely in favor of direct prediction from a decoupled classification and regression head \cite{geYOLOXExceedingYOLO2021}. Finally, the NMS post-processing step was removed from inference by consistent dual label assignment during training \cite{wangYOLOv10RealTimeEndtoEnd2024}. Reliably detecting several small objects clustered in one image nonetheless remains unresolved across the generations of the family \cite{nazirYouOnlyLook2023}.

An early design in this lineage serves as a direct precedent for the taxonomy-aware inference discussed in \Cref{sec:lit_md_speciesnet}: a hierarchical \textit{WordTree} label space that enables a single detector to combine detection-only and classification-only training data across a taxonomy \cite{redmonYOLO9000BetterFaster2017}.

Licensing, not architecture, determines which generation this work can train. \textit{YOLOv5} can only be used commercially up to commit \texttt{5cdad89}. Later commits require additional licensing that is incompatible with the requirement that the resulting weights be usable in a commercial product \cite{ultralyticsComprehensiveGuideUltralytics}. Therefore, every newer Ultralytics release is ruled out, independent of any architectural or accuracy comparison. \textit{YOLOv5s} is the architecture trained throughout \Cref{sec:augmentation_setups}. There is no peer-reviewed paper for any of the releases from YOLOv5 onward, so citations here are to codebases, release notes, or independent architectural analyses.

The nano-scale variants most relevant to embedded deployment are in the $2.6$--$3.2\,\mathrm{M}$-parameter range (\Cref{sec:target_class_definition}). No paper in the surveyed literature establishes a ceiling for class count versus accuracy for backbones of that size. The closest indirect evidence fixes the class count and varies the backbone size: a $0.99\,\mathrm{M}$-parameter detector achieves an average precision of $30.6\%$ on COCO, compared to $25.3\%$ for a $0.91\,\mathrm{M}$-parameter detector \cite{yuPPPicoDetBetterRealTime2021,geYOLOXExceedingYOLO2021}. That establishes discriminative capacity as a binding constraint for sub-million-parameter backbones in general, but not how that capacity trades off against a growing number of classes. The $200$--$250$-class ceiling adopted in \Cref{sec:target_class_definition} is therefore this work's own extrapolation from that general capacity-accuracy relationship, not a literature-established figure, and it is treated as such throughout.

The established alternative for wildlife imagery is worth examining directly due to the limitations of what one small detector can resolve.

\subsection{The Detector-Plus-Classifier Ensemble}\label{sec:lit_md_speciesnet}\label{sec:lit_yolo_vs_ensemble}

A second architecture takes an opposite structural approach: a species-agnostic localizer that feeds a separate, much larger species classifier. This work uses the localizer as a pseudo-labeling and quality-filtering tool inside its own pipeline rather than as a deployment target (\Cref{sec:staged_filtering_pipeline,sec:speciesnet_matching}).

\textit{MegaDetector} detects animals, humans, and vehicles in camera-style imagery without identifying species \cite{beeryEfficientPipelineCamera2019}. Its output crops pass to \textit{SpeciesNet}, a classifier built on an \textit{EfficientNetV2-M} backbone \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024a} and trained, in its largest reported configuration, on roughly $36\,\mathrm{M}$ camera-trap images spanning more than $2{,}000$ taxonomic labels \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024}.

Instead of making a species-level prediction regardless of confidence, the classification stage uses a confidence-gated taxonomic rollup. When the top species-level prediction falls below the confidence threshold, the confidence scores of each taxon are summed upward across the taxonomy until a higher clade (genus, family, etc.) clears it. Thus, a low-confidence prediction degrades into a coarser but still informative answer instead of an incorrect species identification \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024}. Similarly, a complementary geofencing step rolls up in the same way whenever the top prediction falls outside a region-specific allow list \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024}.

This arrangement is the practical accuracy reference for recognizing wildlife species from camera-style imagery due to two properties. Cropping to a detected animal before classifying improves macro-averaged F1 by roughly $25\%$ over whole-image classification on a large, long-tailed camera-trap dataset, and the result reproduces on two further public datasets \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024}. The rollup then ensures that classifications that are too uncertain to identify a species correctly still return the correct genus or family. A single detector trained directly on species-level classes does not have this capability by default. Below its confidence threshold a detection is simply dropped or misclassified, unless an intermediate taxonomic level is deliberately engineered into the label space or the loss, which \textit{WordTree} showed to be possible in principle for a detector \cite{redmonYOLO9000BetterFaster2017}.

The cost is structural, and the separate designs of the two components do not hide it. Both a full detector and a large classification backbone run, and the classifier runs once per detected crop. Therefore, total inference time scales with the number of animals in the frame instead of staying constant. \textit{EfficientNetV2-M} on its own is far larger than the nano-scale backbones that the \textit{QCS605}'s Hexagon 685 DSP is sized for. This comparison does not even take into account the detector backbone or the per-crop multiplier.

Therefore, the choice between the two architectures is based on deployability rather than accuracy. The ensemble is more accurate and degrades more gracefully. However, fitting it to the target hardware would require compressing two networks that were trained separately, each with its own accuracy-versus-size curve. A single end-to-end detector trained on the 225-class taxonomy sacrifices both the accuracy margin and the taxonomic fallback. However, it runs at a fixed per-frame cost and leaves one network to compress and export. This work trains the single detector, shifting the accuracy burden to the training data. A nano-scale detector asked to resolve 225 fine-grained classes must be shown enough examples of each class.

\section{Synthetic Training Data for Visual Recognition}\label{sec:lit_synthetic_data}

This work introduces synthetic imagery as a solution for classes with insufficient real-image data for training (\Cref{sec:synthetic_data_supplementation}). This section begins with the generative models that produce such imagery and then moves on to the sim-to-real gap, which determines whether the imagery can be used for training. Next, it discusses how to measure the quality of the imagery. Finally, it covers the specific role that synthetic imagery can play for long-tail classes.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Generative Models for Image Synthesis}\label{sec:lit_generative_models}

For a decade, deep generative image modeling was dominated by adversarial training, in which a generator and a discriminator are optimized against each other in a minimax game \cite{goodfellowGenerativeAdversarialNetworks2014}. The objective is notoriously unstable to optimize, and it admits a degenerate mode in which the generator collapses many inputs onto a single output.

Diffusion models approach synthesis differently. Instead of learning a single feed-forward noise-to-image mapping, they learn to reverse a fixed Markov chain that gradually corrupts an image into noise, trained by predicting the noise added at each step of the forward corruption \cite{hoDenoisingDiffusionProbabilistic2020}. The resulting samples surpass those from adversarial training in quality without inheriting its instability, albeit at the cost of many sequential network evaluations per sample.

Two developments turned this into the text-to-image generation used in this work. \textit{Latent diffusion}, the basis of \textit{Stable Diffusion}, runs the diffusion process in the compressed latent space of a separately pretrained perceptual-compression autoencoder, and introduces cross-attention layers into the denoising network so that arbitrary conditioning modalities, text among them, can be attached \cite{rombachHighResolutionImageSynthesis2022}. This cuts training and inference cost by roughly an order of magnitude against pixel-space diffusion while retaining competitive synthesis quality, and it is what makes locally-hosted image generation practical on a single accelerator at all.

\textit{Classifier-free guidance} governs the trade-off between sample fidelity and sample diversity \cite{hoClassifierFreeDiffusionGuidance2022}. A single network is jointly trained as both a conditional and an unconditional diffusion model by randomly dropping the conditioning signal during training. At sampling time, the two score estimates are linearly extrapolated. Increasing the guidance scale pushes samples toward the conditioning text and away from the unconditional prior, thereby sharpening prompt adherence at the expense of variety. Nearly every text-conditioned diffusion model now offers this as a sampling parameter, and it is one of the generation settings this work has to fix (\Cref{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}).

A second axis, orthogonal to the architectural one, is the amount of natural language information on which a model can be conditioned. An open diffusion checkpoint inherits its text-conditioning limit from the text encoder it uses. Checkpoints conditioned on \textit{CLIP}, a contrastively trained image and text encoder, are capped at $77$ tokens by its transformer text branch \cite{radfordLearningTransferableVisual2021}. This is an architectural limit rather than a recommendation. Later checkpoints pair a CLIP-family encoder with \textit{T5}, an encoder-decoder transformer pretrained at a maximum sequence length of $512$ tokens \cite{raffelExploringLimitsTransfer2023}. This increases the usable prompt length by roughly an order of magnitude and measurably improves adherence on complex, highly detailed prompts while only slightly affecting general aesthetic quality \cite{esserScalingRectifiedFlow2024}.

Large proprietary providers moved in the opposite direction from these openly released, self-hostable checkpoints. Hosted text-to-image and image-editing services, such as \textit{Gemini} image generation and the \textit{GPT-image} family whose lineage traces to \textit{DALL-E} \cite{openaiDALLECreatingImages2021}, publish neither their architectures nor their text-encoder budgets, and in practice accept prompts far longer than any locally-hosted checkpoint can consume.

For this work, the difference between a hosted model's effectively unbounded prompt and an open checkpoint's fixed token ceiling is not a peripheral detail. A prompt written for one cannot be reused verbatim for the other without altering the surviving information. This is why prompt length is treated as a separately controlled experimental factor in the generator comparison of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison} rather than as a fixed quantity.

\subsection{The Sim-to-Real Gap in Synthetic Training Data}\label{sec:lit_sim_to_real}

Models trained purely on synthetic imagery can transfer to real-world perception tasks, provided the synthetic data is constructed with transfer in mind. This concept was established in robotics and computer vision long before the existence of diffusion-based image generation.

\textit{Domain randomization} revealed this in the starkest form. An object-localization network that was trained exclusively on low-fidelity renders with randomized, non-photorealistic textures, lighting, camera poses, and distractor objects reached centimeter-level, real-world localization accuracy without any pretraining on real images \cite{tobinDomainRandomizationTransferring2017}. The hypothesis is that, with sufficient variability in the simulated distribution, the real world appears to the model as just another random variation rather than a distinct domain. This runs counter to the intuition that closing the sim-to-real gap requires photorealism. When extended to full 2D detection, a domain-randomized detector that was trained using non-photorealistic renders over random real-photo backgrounds matched or surpassed the performance of a detector that was trained using a much higher-fidelity synthetic dataset that was designed to resemble the real \textit{KITTI} test distribution. Fine-tuning afterward on a modest amount of real data improved the results beyond those achieved by training on real data alone \cite{tremblayTrainingDeepNetworks2018}.

Generative models pose a harder version of the same problem, because the synthetic distribution is now defined implicitly by a learned generator instead of by an explicit, controllable rendering pipeline. Diffusion output layered on top of a strong pretrained feature extractor measurably improves zero-shot classification, but the same synthetic data used to train a classifier from scratch underperforms real data by a wide margin, requiring roughly five times as many synthetic images to match what real images of the same classes achieve \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023}. A strong pretrained representation can partially absorb the gap between generated and real imagery. Raw supervised training cannot.

The gap narrows substantially when the generator itself is fine-tuned on the target domain rather than merely prompted. A diffusion model fine-tuned on the target label space produces images good enough to train a classifier that scores competitively on the real validation set, though still short of real-data training \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. Naively prompting an off-the-shelf model with short class-name prompts, by contrast, produces a very poor classifier, and contemporaneous work found that unmodified open-checkpoint output added nothing at all when mixed into real training data \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. Raw visual fidelity of the generator is therefore not the operative variable. Alignment between the generator's learned distribution and the target label space and appearance distribution is.

Constraining generation to stay close to a real reference image is a third position on the same axis. Editing real images semantically, rather than generating them from scratch, and using per-class embeddings learned from as few as one labeled example, outperforms both geometric augmentation and unconstrained diffusion augmentation in fine-grained few-shot settings \cite{trabuccoEffectiveDataAugmentation2025}. Keeping the object's own pixels real and synthesizing only the context around it sharpens the caveat. In earlier work by the present author, a detector trained on real car cutouts pasted onto generated backgrounds improved substantially over the unaugmented baseline, but a control filling the same canvas with per-pixel random noise instead of generated context did at least as well at every substitution ratio tested \cite{hedrichGeneratingSyntheticDatasets2026}. The gain followed from the shifted relative-object-size distribution, not from the realism of the surrounding pixels. That study covered one class, one detector, and one evaluation dataset, so it supports only the narrow conclusion that generator realism cannot be assumed to be the operative variable in downstream detector accuracy.

Three failure modes recur across this body of work. The first is a persistent texture and lighting domain shift between generated pixels and real sensor imagery, present even after substantial fine-tuning effort \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. The second is a label- and context-alignment gap, in which a generator's default visual associations for a class or scene diverge from the target task's actual distribution unless explicitly corrected \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023,aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. The third compounds across successive generations rather than within one: \textit{model collapse}, the degeneration of a generative model's learned distribution when it is recursively retrained on data sampled from itself, which first loses the distributional tails and eventually converges on a low-variance, degenerate output \cite{shumailovAIModelsCollapse2024}.

These are the reasons why a detector in this work is never judged on synthetic images alone (\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}). A headline metric computed on synthetic imagery alone could appear artificially strong because it cannot probe the domain shift and alignment gaps in question. To treat synthetic imagery as a supplement whose reliability is verified, one must first know what can be measured about generated imagery.

\subsection{Evaluating Synthetic Image Quality}\label{sec:lit_synthetic_evaluation}

Historically, generated imagery has been assessed numerically through statistics computed in a pretrained image classifier's feature space rather than by comparing it pixel by pixel against a reference.

The \textit{Inception Score} (IS) is the earliest form of this concept \cite{salimansImprovedTechniquesTraining2016}. Generated images are propagated through an ImageNet-pretrained classifier. The IS rewards a low-entropy, per-image label distribution, meaning each image is confidently assigned to one class. However, it requires a high-entropy label distribution across the entire sample, meaning the sample is diverse. The IS does not compare generated samples to any statistic of the real data distribution. Directly optimizing the IS produces artifacts similar to adversarial examples instead of genuine quality improvements \cite{salimansImprovedTechniquesTraining2016}.

\textit{Fréchet Inception Distance} (FID) closes exactly that gap \cite{heuselGANsTrainedTwo2018}. Real and generated images pass through the same pretrained activations, each set is modeled as a multivariate Gaussian, and FID is the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians, $\mathrm{FID} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|^2 + \mathrm{Tr}(C_r + C_g - 2(C_r C_g)^{1/2})$. It increases monotonically under independently controlled image disturbances such as noise, blur, implanted occlusion, and cross-dataset contamination, a consistency property IS does not reliably exhibit \cite{heuselGANsTrainedTwo2018}.

\textit{Kernel Inception Distance} (KID) fixes FID's principal statistical weakness \cite{binkowskiDemystifyingMMDGANs2021}. FID's Gaussian-moment estimator is biased, no unbiased estimator of FID exists, and the estimator needs a large sample before it converges. That bias is not guaranteed to be consistent across different pairs of distributions, so two FID values computed at the same sample size can rank two generators in the wrong order. KID instead computes an unbiased squared maximum mean discrepancy between the same representations under a polynomial kernel, converging to its asymptotic value even at small sample sizes while keeping FID's sensitivity to the same disturbances. Both are computed for every candidate generator in this work (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison}).

Feature-space metrics do not reliably track human perceptual judgments. Under a structured, blind, multi-rater protocol based in psychophysics, human deception rates showed no significant correlation with FID on face generation and only a weak, model-dependent correlation on conditional generation \cite{zhouHYPEBenchmarkHuman2019}. Using the same protocol, the strongest generator of its generation deceived only marginally more raters than the chance baseline that defines indistinguishability. The claim this work takes from that result is the divergence itself: automatic and human evaluation axes must be triangulated, not substituted for one another.

Realism is also not the only thing that can fail. An image can be photorealistic and still not depict what its prompt asked for, a failure mode that IS, FID, and KID cannot detect at all, since none of them examines the conditioning text. \textit{CLIPScore} measures it directly and without reference captions, by passing image and prompt through CLIP's respective encoders and rescaling their cosine similarity \cite{hesselCLIPScoreReferencefreeEvaluation2021}. Despite requiring no ground truth, it correlates better with human judgments of caption quality than the $n$-gram-overlap metrics it was benchmarked against, which shows that a general-purpose vision-language embedding transfers to quality assessment without task-specific supervision \cite{hesselCLIPScoreReferencefreeEvaluation2021}.

None of these axes measures what ultimately matters for a downstream detector: whether a model trained on generated imagery performs well on real data. The \textit{Classification Accuracy Score} (CAS) directly operationalizes the idea of training on synthetic data and testing on real data \cite{ravuriClassificationAccuracyScore2019}. A classifier is trained exclusively on class-conditional samples from the generative model and then evaluated on a real, held-out test set. Its real-test accuracy is the score. When applied to a generator whose IS and FID approached those of real data at the time, CAS revealed a significant top-1 accuracy discrepancy compared to real-data training, and models with notably poorer FID and IS outperformed it. CAS also isolated individual classes on which the downstream classifier scored zero on the real validation set, a failure a single global FID number hides entirely \cite{ravuriClassificationAccuracyScore2019}.

Three evaluation axes therefore exist and demonstrably disagree: structured blind human rating, automatic distributional and embedding-based proxies, and downstream task utility. This work evaluates its candidate generators along all three and treats none as decisive (\Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability}), because a generator strong on one need not be strong on another. What that leaves open is which classes synthetic imagery should be spent on in the first place.

\subsection{Synthetic Data for Long-Tail Recognition}\label{sec:lit_synthetic_long_tail}

Real-world visual recognition datasets rarely have a uniform class distribution. There are two types of remedies: class re-balancing, which covers re-sampling and cost-sensitive re-weighting, and information augmentation, which covers transfer learning and data augmentation \cite{zhangDeepLongTailedLearning2023}. Both types predate the current generation of generative image models and were designed to optimize the use of existing samples rather than generate new ones.

Re-sampling addresses the imbalance at the data level. Duplicating minority-class examples verbatim leads to overfitting in a narrow decision region. Therefore, the foundational method of this family synthesizes new minority-class points by interpolating between a sample and its nearest same-class neighbors in feature space \cite{chawlaSMOTESyntheticMinority2002}. Interpolation can only recombine the visual information that the dataset already contains: the same pose, background, and lighting variations present in the neighbors between which it interpolates. However, it cannot supply a camera angle, habitat, or lighting condition that no real photograph of the class exhibits.

Cost-sensitive re-weighting works on the loss instead of the data. Weighting each class's loss by the inverse of its effective number of samples improves long-tailed accuracy across image benchmarks \cite{cuiClassBalancedLossBased2019}. This quantity is derived from the diminishing marginal information that additional, likely-overlapping samples of a well-represented class contribute. Deferring the enforcement of a proportionally larger decision-boundary margin for rarer classes to a later training stage, which avoids the optimization difficulties that re-weighting from the start is known to cause, achieves the same result \cite{caoLearningImbalancedDatasets2019}. Both methods change how existing images are counted during training but do not affect whether there are enough images to represent a class's visual diversity.

Re-balancing may not even be the right target. The best feature representations are learned under plain instance-balanced sampling, with no re-balancing during representation learning at all, and long-tailed performance is recovered afterward by re-adjusting the classifier alone \cite{kangDecouplingRepresentationClassifier2020}. That reframes the practical failure of long-tailed recognition as a biased decision boundary rather than a poorly learned representation. It presupposes, however, that a class's representation was learnable from its available images to begin with, and offers no remedy for a class whose real-image pool is too small for any sampling scheme to recover from.

The same limit applies to the augmentation methods examined alongside them. Both transfer-based and non-transfer variants operate in feature space using samples that the dataset already contains, and none synthesizes new raw images from an external generative prior. The survey is explicit that how best to augment for long-tailed learning remains an open question, and warns separately that class-agnostic augmentation helps head classes as much as tail classes and therefore delivers no relative benefit where it is needed most \cite{zhangDeepLongTailedLearning2023}.

Rather than any single method, it is this contrast that motivates the band structure adopted in this work (\Cref{sec:band_structure}). Re-sampling, cost-sensitive re-weighting, and feature-space augmentation redistribute the information that an existing image pool of a class already contains, so their effectiveness is limited by the visual diversity of that pool. Image-level generative synthesis is a categorically different intervention because it manufactures new appearance content, such as camera angle, habitat, lighting, and pose, that real images of a data-poor class cannot provide. Synthetic supplementation is therefore reserved for classes with a real image pool deemed too limited to provide that diversity. It is deliberately withheld from classes with a more extensive real image pool so they can serve as a real-data control at a matched training set size.

\section{Model Compression for Embedded Inference}\label{sec:lit_kd_and_embedded}

Getting a detector trained this way onto the target device raises two further questions: whether a large model's knowledge can be transferred into a small one, and what governs how fast the small one runs once deployed.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Knowledge Distillation Fundamentals}\label{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation}

The process of compressing a model by training a compact model to reproduce the predictions of a larger model rather than its original hard labels predates the current generation of deep learning \cite{buciluaModelCompression2006}. The now-standard form of this technique involves raising the temperature $T$ of the teacher's softmax before computing the student's training targets \cite{hintonDistillingKnowledgeNeural2015}.

Softening the teacher's output reveals the relative probabilities assigned to \emph{incorrect} classes. These probabilities encode a similarity structure over the label space that a one-hot ground-truth label cannot. For example, an image of a wolf may carry a small probability of being a dog, but mistaking it for a car is far less probable. For a taxonomy of 225 visually confusable mammals, that similarity structure is the exact information that a hard label discards. The student is trained on a weighted combination of two cross-entropy terms: one against the teacher's softened targets, computed at the same high temperature, and one against the ground-truth labels at a temperature of one. The soft-target term's gradient is rescaled by $T^2$ so that the two terms remain comparable as $T$ varies.

Distillation strategies are organized according to which part of the teacher provides supervision \cite{gouKnowledgeDistillationSurvey2021}. \emph{Response-based} knowledge matches only the teacher's final-layer outputs. \emph{Feature-based} knowledge additionally supervises the student's intermediate representations, in its original form by mapping a chosen intermediate student layer onto a teacher hint layer through a learned regressor, which permits students that are deeper and thinner than the teacher rather than merely smaller \cite{romeroFitNetsHintsThin2015}. \emph{Relation-based} knowledge matches structural relations between samples or feature-map pairs instead of individual predictions.

It is not straightforward to transplant the classification recipe onto a detector. A detector's output involves dense per-location classification and regression predictions in the presence of extreme foreground-background imbalance. Distilling teacher and student features uniformly across this imbalance is empirically counterproductive. Distilling the foreground and background regions with equal weighting yields \emph{worse} results than distilling either region separately because the mismatch between the teacher's and student's attention is concentrated disproportionately in the foreground \cite{yangFocalGlobalKnowledge2022}. Focal and Global Distillation (FGD) splits the feature map into foreground and background regions using ground-truth boxes instead. Teacher-derived spatial and channel attention masks then focus the student's limited capacity on the teacher's most salient pixels and channels, and a global relational term built from pixel-pair correlations recovers the cross-region context a hard split discards \cite{yangFocalGlobalKnowledge2022}.

The general point is important for interpreting the distillation comparison of this work (\Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded}). While plain, spatially unaware logit or feature matching is adequate for image classification, a detection head requires this kind of foreground/background- and attention-aware treatment to perform comparably. A naive transplant of the classification recipe should not be expected to match a detection-specific scheme. A result from one is evidence about that transplant rather than about distillation in general.

A smaller student is only useful if it also runs fast enough on the target device, which is a question about numerical precision, export format, and measurement protocol rather than about training.

\subsection{Embedded Inference Feasibility}\label{sec:lit_embedded_inference}

How on-device inference is measured is itself an active concern. An architecture-neutral methodology for comparing inference latency and throughput across heterogeneous hardware and software stacks \cite{reddiMLPerfInferenceBenchmark2020} has been adapted to microcontroller- and DSP-class devices as \textit{MLPerf Tiny}, which reports accuracy, latency, and energy together and standardizes on a common reference inference stack, so that cross-device comparisons are not confounded by inconsistent runtime choices \cite{banburyMLPerfTinyBenchmark2021}. Its measurement protocol deliberately scopes the timed window to model inference alone, explicitly excluding any pre- or post-processing performed around it. This work's own runtime check (\Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}) observes the same methodological control, and additionally excludes one-time effects such as initial model loading and cache and frequency-scaling warm-up.

A second variable that such a benchmark must hold fixed is the export format and runtime. The case for a portable, interpreter-based deployment framework instead of a bespoke, per-device, compiled pipeline rests on the fragmented nature of the embedded ecosystem across instruction sets, the absence of operating system and dynamic memory support, and the need for vendor-specific kernel optimizations \cite{davidTensorFlowLiteMicro2021}. The same work treats interpreter behavior under multi-threaded and multi-core execution as a primary design concern rather than an afterthought. Therefore, thread and core affinity choices on a multi-core ARM system-on-chip, such as the \textit{AX Visio}'s, are a variable that must be held constant across repeated measurements, along with the export format itself (whether \textit{ONNX}, \textit{TFLite}, \textit{NCNN}, or a vendor-specific format, such as Qualcomm's \textit{SNPE} \texttt{.dlc}).

Numerical precision is the cheapest optimization available, and the foundational literature on it targets exactly this work's hardware family. Post-training quantization (PTQ) involves quantizing a trained network's weights and activations from 32-bit floating point to 8-bit integers after training. This technique maintains classification accuracy within $2\%$ of the floating-point baseline across a range of convolutional architectures when weights are quantized per channel and activations per layer \cite{krishnamoorthiQuantizingDeepConvolutional2018}. The resulting speedup is benchmarked at up to $10\times$ over floating-point CPU inference on Qualcomm fixed-point SIMD DSPs of the same class as the \textit{Hexagon 685} \cite{krishnamoorthiQuantizingDeepConvolutional2018}.

Quantization-aware training (QAT) takes it a step further by simulating quantization during training. It inserts fake quantization nodes into the computation graph so that the weights and activation ranges can adapt to quantization noise before it is applied during inference \cite{jacobQuantizationTrainingNeural2018}. That work frames integer-arithmetic-only hardware such as the Qualcomm Hexagon as its target deployment setting, and reports that QAT narrows the residual accuracy gap to the floating-point baseline further than PTQ alone. The practical distinction lies in the trade-off between cost and accuracy: PTQ requires only a trained model and a small calibration set, whereas QAT necessitates a full retraining process. For the \textit{QCS605}, the vendor toolchain that implements both is the Snapdragon Neural Processing Engine (SNPE) together with the AI Model Efficiency Toolkit (AIMET) \cite{QualcommAimet2026}. Here, it is cited to identify the toolchain rather than as an independent technical source.

This work surveys quantization in that context, but does not implement PTQ, QAT, or any other compression technique. The result reported in \Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark} is a single runtime measurement of the unoptimized, full-precision model. Thus, the accelerated integer path targeted by this literature is surveyed here, but not exercised there.

Whatever precision the deployed model runs at, its accuracy still has to be reported in a form that a 225-class, look-alike-heavy taxonomy makes legible.

\section{Evaluation Metrics for Fine-Grained Wildlife Detection}\label{sec:lit_evaluation_metrics}

Reporting a single mean average precision number is sufficient for a generic 80-class benchmark but not for a 225-class, visually confusable, dual-domain wildlife taxonomy. For example, a model that mistakes a plains zebra for a Grévy's zebra has made a fundamentally different error than a model that misses the animal entirely. Additionally, a model evaluated on a synthetic image has been probed under a different data-generating process than a model evaluated on a field photograph.

This section reviews the literature that informs the evaluation design of the work. It begins with the general-purpose detection metrics by which any object detector is judged, then moves on to the diagnostic instruments developed to understand \textit{why} a detector fails. Finally, it addresses the question of how to interpret a test set that mixes real and synthetic imagery. The final subsection directly anchors the evaluation methodology adopted in \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework} in the literature.

\subsection{General Object Detection Metrics}\label{sec:lit_detection_metrics}

The dominant evaluation protocol for object detection was established by the \textit{PASCAL VOC} challenge \cite{everinghamPascalVisualObject2010}. A detection counts as correct if its bounding box overlaps a ground-truth box above a fixed \textit{Intersection-over-Union} (IoU) threshold, the ratio of the intersection of the two boxes to their union. That threshold is conventionally set to $0.5$, a deliberately low value chosen to absorb the inherent subjectivity of drawing a tight box around a non-convex object such as an animal with limbs extended \cite{everinghamPascalVisualObject2010}.

Each prediction above the confidence threshold is either a true positive or a false positive. From these, precision (the fraction of correct predictions) and recall (the fraction of ground-truth objects detected) are functions of the threshold. \textit{Average Precision} (AP) summarizes the resulting precision-recall curve as the mean interpolated precision at a fixed set of recall levels, and mean average precision (mAP) averages AP across classes \cite{everinghamPascalVisualObject2010}. This formulation was adopted because a simple accuracy count is misleading under the heavy imbalance between the small number of true objects and the very large number of candidate regions a detector must reject, and it has remained the basis of essentially every detection benchmark since \cite{everinghamPascalVisualObject2010,zouObjectDetection202023}.

Two refinements produced the metric vector used throughout modern detection research. A fixed IoU of $0.5$ rewards coarse localization, because it cannot distinguish a tightly fitted box from a loose one as long as both clear the bar. Averaging AP over ten IoU thresholds spanning $0.5$ to $0.95$ in steps of $0.05$, denoted AP@[.5:.95], as the \textit{COCO} benchmark does, rewards progressively tighter localization instead \cite{linMicrosoftCOCOCommon2015,zouObjectDetection202023}. The single-threshold AP50 and the stricter AP75 are retained beside it as complementary operating points, the first closer to the original PASCAL protocol and the second probing high-precision localization specifically \cite{zouObjectDetection202023}. Separately, AP credits a detector only for the boxes it does emit, so it says nothing about ground-truth objects that are never proposed at all. \textit{Average Recall} (AR) measures that upper bound on achievable recall independently of the confidence ranking, reported at a fixed budget of the top $1$, $10$, or $100$ detections per image \cite{zouObjectDetection202023}.

The resulting twelve-statistic vector covers AP@[.5:.95], AP50, AP75, AP split by object size, and AR at the three detection budgets split the same way. This work retains it unchanged as its own baseline reporting format (\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}), because it is the bridge that keeps the work's numbers legible against published results. It is nonetheless too coarse on its own to answer the questions a 225-class, look-alike-heavy wildlife taxonomy raises.

\subsection{Hierarchical Error Decomposition}\label{sec:lit_hierarchical_evaluation}\label{sec:lit_error_decomposition}

A single flat AP computed over all 225 classes cannot distinguish between two very different types of errors. One type of error is finding an animal and naming the wrong species within a visually confusable group. For example, a plains zebra could be scored as a Grévy's zebra. The other type of error is naming a completely unrelated class. For example, a wolf could be scored as a squirrel. Additionally, AP does not explain \textit{why} a detector underperforms. It may fail to localize objects, localize them but assign the wrong label, or duplicate detections on the same object. From a practical-deployment standpoint, these failures are not equivalent, and they call for different fixes.

Prior work supplies the precedent for both distinctions \cite{hoiemDiagnosingErrorObject2012}. Each false positive falls into one of four mutually exclusive categories: localization error, confusion with a semantically similar object, confusion with a dissimilar labeled object, or confusion with unlabeled background. A localization error occurs when a detection overlaps a true object of the correct class with insufficient IoU. It also includes duplicate detections on an already-detected object. Similarity is defined by small sets of semantically related categories, such as all vehicles or all animals. These sets are fixed \textit{a priori} based on domain knowledge about which categories are related before any detector is run and are never read off a trained model's confusion matrix afterward.

Two findings from that decomposition are relevant to this work. Together, within-group confusion and pure localization error account for the vast majority of high-confidence false positives, far more than confusion involving dissimilar or unlabeled background clutter. Reporting the AP improvement that would result from eliminating each category in isolation reveals which failure mode is worth addressing, a detail that would be missed by looking at a single aggregate number. This work expects within-group confusion to dominate among the zebra, elephant, lynx-caracal, and gazelle-antelope look-alike clusters of the target taxonomy.

The a-priori requirement is the load-bearing part for this work's own grouping strategy (\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}). A taxonomic or curated visual-similarity rollup, decided independently of any candidate model's behavior, is admissible. A grouping mined from a model's own confusion matrix is circular, because it would define the very categories that model is then evaluated against.

The literature surveyed for this work contains no papers that perform such a rollup at the species, genus, and family levels, nor any that formally compare a taxonomy-driven grouping with an empirical-confusion-driven one as competing evaluation designs. The closest available reference is the aforementioned a-priori-grouping principle, which this work expands from a few hand-picked semantic sets to a complete taxonomic hierarchy. The four-way decomposition is adopted alongside the principle as a corroborating instrument to check whether the expected accuracy loss between coarse and fine granularity levels is due to classification confusion among look-alike species, as expected, or to hidden localization or duplicate detection problems. Modern off-the-shelf toolboxes use the same partition with an additional category for missed ground truth. However, the literature review for this work could not find a verifiable citation for the specific implementation used in \Cref{chapter:results}. Therefore, the method is anchored in its original source rather than in a named downstream implementation.

Both instruments still presuppose a single, homogeneous test set.

\subsection{Evaluating Across a Real/Synthetic Test-Domain Split}\label{sec:lit_real_synthetic_evaluation}

So far, every evaluation protocol has been computed over one homogeneous test set of real photographs. Instead, this work's dataset ships two test domains: an uneven, per-class real test set, and a class-balanced $225\times50$ synthetic test set (\Cref{sec:synthetic_data_supplementation}).

When synthetic imagery is involved in training, the standard practice is to evaluate strictly on real, established test sets rather than blend synthetic images into the test condition itself. Work addressing the same sim-to-real problem that this work faces evaluates its detector exclusively on the real-world \textit{KITTI} test set and never incorporates synthetic renders into the test condition, even though a synthetic dataset rendered to match the test domain was available for comparison \cite{tremblayTrainingDeepNetworks2018}. Similarly, work evaluating the usability of modern generative model output as training data benchmarks its synthetically trained models against standard, unmodified real-world test sets rather than blended variants. The resulting gaps are attributed to a domain gap between generated and real image distributions \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023}.

The general problem of a mismatch between the distribution on which a model is evaluated and the distribution it will actually encounter is the subject of substantial domain-generalization literature \cite{zhouDomainGeneralizationSurvey2023}. This literature traces back to early work establishing that benchmark datasets themselves carry systematic biases that limit how their measured performance transfers \cite{UnbiasedLookDataset}. One instance of this is a synthetic-to-real train/test shift.

However, none of this literature treats a synthetic partition as a component to be incorporated into a single headline test metric alongside real data. When synthetic data is included in a test protocol, it is either the sole test domain, isolating a specific capability, or kept rigidly separate from the real test condition so that the domain-shift gap remains measurable.

Therefore, the surveyed literature offers \textbf{no established precedent} for the two specific choices that this work makes: reporting a headline detection metric computed over the union of a real and synthetic test set and using a class-balanced synthetic test set as a diagnostic instrument for classes where real test data is scarce or unreliable. These choices are both argued for on their own terms in \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}, which also defines the real-only breakout reported alongside the mixed figure and the real-versus-synthetic difference that decides whether the evaluation axes are revised. The above literature provides context for that argument, showing what the established alternative looks like and why departing from it requires justification. However, it does not endorse a mixed real-and-synthetic headline metric itself.
