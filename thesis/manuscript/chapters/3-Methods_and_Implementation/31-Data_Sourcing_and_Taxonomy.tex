\section{Dataset Sourcing}\label{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy}

Before any imagery could be gathered, the set of species that the detector was expected to recognize had to be determined, since this set determines what counts as a usable image and how large a corpus can be obtained. This section derives the class taxonomy, assesses candidate image sources for licensing compatibility and suitability, and summarizes the results of the sourcing effort.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Target Class Definition}\label{sec:target_class_definition}

The class universe originates from the taxonomy of \textit{SpeciesNet}, a published species classifier \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024a}. Of its $3{,}537$ labels, approximately $1{,}415$ are mammal taxa. Since the AX Visio binocular targets non-bird mammals, the taxonomy was restricted to that subset. Then, the product owner filtered it down to $483$ candidate labels.  % resources/refineLabels.py
The applied criterion is observability rather than taxonomy. What survives is the set of diurnal land mammals that a user can plausibly photograph. Visually inseparable congeners are merged instead of being dropped.

The decision of whether a species could be supported by imagery was made separately based on the number of images surveyed on \textit{GBIF} for each taxon \cite{GBIF}. At least $300$ images were required for inclusion.  % data/gbif/metadata/GBIF_image_counts_v1.csv
The survey caps at approximately $300$ observations per taxon. Of the $479$ taxa surveyed, $125$ sit exactly at that ceiling. Thus, a count at the cap means only \enquote{at least $300$}. In contrast, a low count is evidence of genuine scarcity. This asymmetry makes the measure usable as a proxy for rarity in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_classes}.

A list of $483$ classes exceeds the capacity of the target hardware. A nano-scale detector in the $1$--$3\,\mathrm{M}$-parameter range spreads its discriminative capacity too thinly once the label space grows beyond roughly $200$--$300$ fine-grained classes, which fixed an approximate ceiling of $200$--$250$ classes. Birds, bats, and marine mammals other than pinnipeds were excluded outright, while domestic animals were deliberately kept.

The final list includes $225$ classes across three taxonomic ranks: $164$ at the species level, $41$ at the genus level (where the species of a genus cannot be reliably separated in a daylight image), and $20$ at the family level or above. \Cref{tab:class-taxonomy-orders} breaks them down by taxonomic order. Whether each of those classes can be filled with usable imagery depends on the available sources.

\subsection{Licensing Assessment of Candidate Sources}\label{sec:candidate_sources_licensing}

With the class list finalized, candidate image sources were evaluated based on two criteria: the commercial usability of the license and the suitability of the imagery for close-to-mid-range daylight observation. Because the trained weights are intended for a commercial product, any dataset with a NonCommercial, NoDerivatives, or ShareAlike clause was excluded. The seven candidates and their verdicts are listed below, with the sources that were used first.

\begin{description}
    \item[iNaturalist Open Data] Used. The photographs are exposed through \textit{GBIF} \cite{GBIF} and an AWS S3 mirror, filtered to observations whose contributors opted into a CC0 or CC-BY license.
    \item[Open Images V7] Used, with taxonomy remapping. The images are licensed CC-BY 2.0 and the boxes CC-BY 4.0. Its \enquote{Mammal} class covers $95{,}335$ boxed images under colloquial rather than species-level labels \cite{kuznetsovaOpenImagesDataset2020}.
    \item[COCO 2017] Used, with strict filtering (\Cref{sec:sourcing_execution}). Its $330\mathrm{k}$ images span $10$ mammal-adjacent classes including \texttt{person} \cite{linMicrosoftCOCOCommon2015}.
    \item[BioCLIP] Weights used as a pseudo-labeler only, not as an image source. The model weights are MIT-licensed, whereas the underlying TreeOfLife-200M image bundle is license-mixed and partially CC-BY-NC-ND \cite{stevensBioCLIPVisionFoundation2024}.
    \item[LILA BC camera-trap archives] License permitted, rejected on suitability. The CDLA-Permissive license would have allowed Snapshot Serengeti, Snapshot Safari, WCS Camera Traps, and Caltech Camera Traps, but their imagery does not match the observation scenario (\Cref{sec:sourcing_execution}) \cite{LILABCLabeled}.
    \item[iNaturalist Competition datasets] Not allowed. The $2021$ edition is taxonomically ideal with $246$ mammal species and $68{,}917$ training images, but it is distributed under a non-commercial academic license \cite{hornINaturalistSpeciesClassification2018}.
    \item[iWildCam, Wildlife-71, ATRW] Not allowed. They contain unverifiable web-scraped images or images contaminated with restricted iNaturalist data.
\end{description}

Source selection also fixed how far each source's own species labels can be trusted, an axis the curation pipeline depends on. \textit{iNaturalist}, \textit{GBIF} and Wikipedia-derived material are label-trusted, since their labels carry an identifiable contributor and community verification. Open Images and the stock-image source are unverified because their labels are colloquial or of unknown origin. An unverified source only contributes to a class's usable pool where an independent classifier corroborates the label. In contrast, a trusted source contributes simply by passing the quality gates (\Cref{sec:data_quality_and_curation}).

\subsection{Sourcing Execution}\label{sec:sourcing_execution}

The initial corpus was assembled from \textit{GBIF} \cite{GBIF} observation records and classified using \textit{SpeciesNet} v$4.0.2$a \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024a}, yielding $66{,}881$ usable images. This corpus was highly uneven across classes: $31$ of the $225$ classes had zero images, and a further $55$ classes had fewer than $10$. Closing that gap required sourcing additional imagery.

Three large camera-trap archives under commercially compatible licenses were scanned against the $225$-class taxonomy and rejected \cite{LILABCLabeled}. Their species coverage overlapped with well-supplied classes and missed scarce ones. Additionally, their imagery is fixed-viewpoint, long-range, and frequently infrared. The \textit{AX Visio}, however, is used for close-to-mid-range daylight observation. No images from these archives were included in the corpus.

\paragraph{Gap-Filling Sources Actually Used}
A metadata-level match against \textit{iNaturalist} Open Data records exposed through \textit{GBIF} reached $221$ of the $225$ classes and $6{,}816{,}962$ photographs. Of these, only $831{,}050$ ($12\%$) carried a commercially safe CC0 or CC-BY license. The CC-BY-NC remainder was excluded under the policy of \Cref{sec:candidate_sources_licensing}. Coverage stayed uneven: white-tailed deer retained $61{,}865$ safe images, while nine classes had none and needed separate sourcing.

Human imagery was withheld from the iNaturalist and GBIF exports for privacy reasons. It was needed as a contextual negative rather than as a wildlife species. Open Images' person category was dominated by portrait photography. Instead, \textit{COCO} 2017 \cite{linMicrosoftCOCOCommon2015} supplied the \texttt{human} class under a filter written for it that constrains subject size, distance from the frame edge, upright aspect, and image sharpness (\Cref{sec:appendix_human_filter}).  % scripts/download_coco_humans.py

After those two steps, the species that were still underrepresented were filled from a Wikimedia Commons category crawl. This crawl was built as a human-in-the-loop instrument rather than as a downloader. It produces a reviewable category tree per label. An automatic filter drops categories whose subject is not a live wild animal (\Cref{sec:appendix_wikimedia_vocabulary}). Only categories that survive review are fetched.

\paragraph{A Divergence from the Stated License Policy}
An audit found that the implemented license allow-list admits ShareAlike variants, and NoDerivatives on the COCO downloads, contrary to the policy stated above. Of the $11{,}066$ Wikimedia images in the final splits, $6{,}222$ carry a ShareAlike license. Therefore, every model reported in \Cref{chapter:results} was trained and evaluated on a corpus containing imagery excluded by the policy, and this finding is recorded as a named limitation (\Cref{sec:limitations}).

The raw corpus contains at least one image for $216$ of the $225$ target classes. After the quality filtering of \Cref{sec:data_quality_and_curation}, \Cref{tab:source-funnel} provides its per-source composition. Coverage remained heavily uneven despite the volume. $83$ classes held at least $1{,}500$ images, while $29$ held fewer than $100$ and a further $58$ fewer than $500$. Two inputs are not wildlife imagery at all: the COCO-derived human class, and $174$ background photographs retained as true negatives, so that the detector sees frames whose correct answer is to predict nothing. Wikipedia species articles were scraped as well, as the textual corpus from which synthetic-image prompts are built (\Cref{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}). This pool still mixes sources of very different reliability, which \Cref{sec:data_quality_and_curation} addresses.
