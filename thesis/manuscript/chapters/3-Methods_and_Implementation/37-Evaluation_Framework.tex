\section{Evaluation Framework}\label{sec:evaluation_framework}

Every model trained in \Cref{sec:model_training_experiment_tracking} is scored on one fixed instrument, and this section defines it. Two properties of this dataset make a single mean-average-precision figure insufficient on its own. Several classes are barely separable in a still frame, so a species-level slip and a wholly unrelated call are errors of very different severity that one number cannot distinguish. And the dataset ships two test domains rather than one, a real held-out set that is uneven across classes and a class-balanced synthetic set, which an aggregate figure silently blends. The literature surveyed in \Cref{sec:lit_evaluation_metrics} supplies the baseline instrument and the diagnostic vocabulary. What it does not supply, as \Cref{sec:lit_real_synthetic_evaluation} states, is a precedent for the headline condition adopted here, which is therefore argued for directly in \Cref{sec:mixed_default}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Evaluation Axes}\label{sec:evaluation_axes}

Four axes describe every number this thesis reports about detection accuracy. \textbf{Granularity} is the class resolution at which a detection is judged correct, at three levels. \texttt{detect} ignores the class entirely and asks only whether an animal was localized. \texttt{coarse} merges visually confusable classes into the groups of \Cref{sec:lookalike_groups}. \texttt{fine} is the full $225$-way judgment. \textbf{Test domain} is which held-out images the metric is computed over, either the mixed, real or synthetic test set of \Cref{sec:mixed_default}. \textbf{Training band} is the per-class property of how a class's training data was composed, Band A through Band D as defined in \Cref{sec:band_structure}. \textbf{Metric vector} is the COCO statistics themselves \cite{linMicrosoftCOCOCommon2015}.

Crossing all four naively produces $3 \times 3 \times 5 \times 12 = 540$ numbers before any per-class table, which is unreportable and, more importantly, mostly uninformative. The design adopted here rests on the observation that the four axes do not play the same role. Band and test domain are the scientific independent variables, since the questions this thesis asks are whether synthetic training data works and whether it transfers to real photographs, so crossing those two is the experiment. Granularity is not a fourth experiment at all but an error decomposition, and the metric vector is a fixed instrument, not a variable to sweep.

That reframing yields the organizing rule for everything reported in \Cref{chapter:results}. One default cell carries the headline. Every diagnostic varies exactly one axis away from it and reports a compact pair of figures, mAP@[.5:.95] and mAP50. The full twelve-statistic vector is filled only in the default cell and once per band, and the interior of the four-dimensional cube is left unfilled and emitted as machine-readable artifacts instead of narrated. The result is roughly four small tables carrying on the order of forty numbers in the body of the thesis. What this deliberately gives up is any figure crossing average recall or the object-size splits with band or granularity, which would answer no question posed here.

Treating granularity as a decomposition rather than as three rival accuracies is what makes the ladder informative. The three levels are nested upper bounds, since a detection judged correct at the fine level is necessarily correct at the coarse and detection levels:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{mAP}_{\text{detect}} \;\geq\; \mathrm{mAP}_{\text{coarse}} \;\geq\; \mathrm{mAP}_{\text{fine}} .
    \label{eq:granularity-ladder}
\end{equation}
The quantities of interest are the two gaps in \Cref{eq:granularity-ladder}. Write $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ for the drop from detection to coarse classification, which is the cost of having to name a group. Write $\Delta_{\text{fine}}$ for the drop from coarse to fine, which is the cost of naming the exact species within that group. A large $\Delta_{\text{fine}}$ beside a small $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ is the signature of the look-alike problem, and is the expected and most defensible failure mode for this dataset. A large $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ instead means cross-group confusion, which is a genuine failure. A low $\mathrm{mAP}_{\text{detect}}$ means the detector cannot localize at all, which is orthogonal to classification.

Two implementation properties keep this affordable and honest. The ladder needs no new metric code. The same scorer runs three times, and only COCO's \texttt{category\_id} fields are rewritten, in the predictions and the ground truth alike, before each pass. Merging classes does introduce an artifact, since two predictions that differed in class become duplicates on one animal once merged, but greedy matching charges the surplus as false positives. The coarse figure is therefore slightly penalized rather than inflated, which is the conservative direction. Separately, inference runs once per component domain and is cached, so each of the dozen reported views is a remap and a filter over stored predictions instead of another forward pass. Without that, the number of views reported here would not be affordable at all.

\subsection{The Mixed Real+Synthetic Default}\label{sec:mixed_default}

Three test domains are distinguished throughout this thesis, and they are named consistently because the reporting rules below depend on telling them apart. The \textbf{real test set} is the held-out real photographs, uneven across classes by construction. The \textbf{synthetic test set} is the balanced $225 \times 50$ held-out set of \Cref{sec:synthetic_test_set}. The \textbf{mixed test set} is the union of the two, scored together as a single set, not averaged after the fact. The default cell that carries the headline number is fine granularity on the mixed test set over all bands, reported with the full twelve-statistic vector.

Three reasons support that default, in ascending order of weight. The fixed $50$ synthetic images per class give every class a consistent, controlled probe. That matters most for the Band A classes, which hold as few as five to thirty real test images and whose real-only per-class accuracy is otherwise dominated by a handful of hard frames. For a well-resourced Band D class, with up to $500$ real test images, the same $50$ images are a small and constant addition that does not move the figure much. And the mixed set always contains the real evidence, so no model in this thesis is ever judged on synthetic images alone.

One property of that construction is acknowledged here instead of hidden. Because the synthetic contribution is fixed while the real contribution varies by two orders of magnitude, the synthetic share of a class's score is inversely related to how much real data that class has. It is dominant for a thin Band A class, which is exactly the stabilization the design wants, and marginal for Band D. The consequence is that the mixed figure's composition differs per class, so the real and synthetic test-image counts are reported per class alongside it and the headline is never presented as a homogeneous measurement.

Two reporting rules follow, and both hold without exception. First, the real-only breakout is reported alongside the mixed headline every time. It is the primary-evaluation figure and the only number in this thesis comparable to a published real-image benchmark, and reporting it beside the headline is what prevents the synthetic component from quietly inflating a result. Second, the paired per-class difference between real and synthetic accuracy is monitored as a standing watchdog. Should that difference show the synthetic component systematically inflating or deflating the mixed figure, the default axes are to be revised. The likely revision is to make the real test set the default again and retain the mixed figure as secondary. \Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} states whether that trigger fired.

A last qualification governs any comparison outside this thesis. A $225$-class fine-grained wildlife mAP is not comparable in absolute terms to a published $80$-class COCO figure. The class count is larger and many of the classes are near-duplicates of one another. A lower fine-grained figure is therefore expected, and is not evidence of a weaker detector. The closest honest analog to a generic detection benchmark is the class-agnostic $\mathrm{mAP}_{\text{detect}}$, which measures localization on the same footing independently of the taxonomy. To keep even that bridge valid, all figures use COCO-standard settings, averaging over IoU thresholds from $0.5$ to $0.95$ with at most $100$ detections per image. The cap was checked rather than assumed: the busiest test image carries $28$ ground-truth boxes, so $100$ is far above the binding value and raising it changed nothing.

\subsection{Look-Alike Groups and Coarse Evaluation}\label{sec:lookalike_groups}

Coarse granularity needs a fixed table mapping each of the $225$ classes to a group, and how that table is built determines whether the coarse figure means anything. Three constructions were available. Grouping classes by the confusions a trained model actually makes is the most faithful to visual confusability and is rejected outright, because a grouping derived from the model under evaluation is circular. Hand-listing the notorious look-alikes targets exactly the intended meaning but is subjective. Rolling each class up to its genus is reproducible, decided before any model runs, and carries no leakage from model behavior. The construction adopted is the taxonomic rollup as the backbone, corrected by a small documented override list. It follows the a-priori grouping principle established by \textcite{hoiemDiagnosingErrorObject2012}, extended from a handful of hand-picked semantic sets to a full taxonomic hierarchy.

Taxonomy is a cheap proxy for visual similarity and it leaks in both directions, which is what the override list exists to correct. Some visually distinct classes share a genus, and some genuine look-alikes span genera. The governing criterion is the one already stated in \Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}. It is a judgment about what counts as a failure rather than a technical rule. Two classes are merged when confusing them in a single still frame is a forgivable error, which belongs in $\Delta_{\text{fine}}$. They are kept apart when confusing them is a real failure, which must surface in $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$. Applying that criterion split two genera, merged one cross-genus cluster, and took the table from $173$ groups with $28$ multi-member groups and $3$ overrides to $174$ groups with $30$ multi-member groups and $8$ overrides.  % reports/lookalike_groups_v2.csv
Where a case is uncertain the default is not to merge, since an unjustified merge silently forgives a real error.

The table is frozen before the first evaluation runs, and the reason is worth making explicit. Any later change to it invalidates every coarse-granularity figure computed up to that point without producing any visible error, so a revision would have to be recorded as a new version and all affected numbers recomputed. The purely taxonomic version is retained unchanged as an audit artifact instead of overwritten, which leaves the effect of the override list itself checkable. The candidate pairs mined during ground-truth review (\Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}) informed which clusters to examine, but did not by themselves define any group.

Empirical confusions do have a legitimate use, after the fact and descriptively only. \Cref{sec:results_lookalike_confusion} compares the within-group confusion the model actually exhibits against this a-priori table, which is a check on the table and not an input to it. Should the two diverge sharply, that divergence is a finding to report and not a reason to re-cut the groups mid-study.

\subsection{Metric Selection}\label{sec:metric_selection}

The COCO twelve-statistic vector surveyed in \Cref{sec:lit_detection_metrics} is retained unchanged as the baseline instrument, because it is the bridge that keeps this thesis's numbers legible against published work. It is filled in the default cell and once per band. Everywhere else the reporting collapses to mAP@[.5:.95] and mAP50, on the reasoning of \Cref{sec:evaluation_axes} that the metric vector is an instrument rather than an axis. All scoring runs through one implementation, validated against the training loop's own evaluator to four decimal places on a representative sample. A figure quoted from a training log and the same figure quoted from an evaluation report are therefore the same measurement.

Four qualifications accompany every number, each addressing a specific way a fine-grained table on an imbalanced test set can mislead. Macro averaging over classes stays primary, as the COCO standard and the class-imbalance-robust choice, with a count-weighted average reported as a sensitivity check, since the two can diverge on a test set this uneven. Bootstrap confidence intervals, resampling images with replacement, cover the headline figure and the band and domain differences. The Band A and Band B test sets are small enough that a difference between two models may not be a difference at all. The $9$ classes holding fewer than $30$ real test images are flagged in every per-class table, and the headline is reported both with and without them so the reader can see the sensitivity directly.  % scripts/training/yolo26n/model_exports/yolo26n-bs32-20260910-212812/evaluation/evaluation_report.json
And a single fixed seed governs every sampled evaluation, so re-running the suite against a new checkpoint yields a directly comparable figure rather than a differently sampled one.

One construction detail affects reproducibility enough to record. The real and synthetic annotation files each number their images from one. Forming the mixed test set therefore offsets the synthetic identifiers past the real maximum before the two are scored together, and verifies that the class tables of the two files are identical. A silent collision here would not raise an error. It would produce a plausible and wrong number.

Two instruments complement the metric vector without extending it. The error-type decomposition anchored in \Cref{sec:lit_error_decomposition} partitions the detector's mistakes into classification, localization, duplicate, background and missed ground truth in one pass. It is used to corroborate that a gap between the coarse and fine levels really is look-alike confusion, rather than a hidden localization or duplication problem. The within-group confusion rate asks, of the detections that are correctly localized and in the right group, what fraction name the wrong species, which is the most directly interpretable answer to whether the model confuses the zebras. Both feed the three-level reporting structure that \Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison} follows: a headline table per model, three diagnostic tables, and the per-class and full-vector material emitted as appendix artifacts.
