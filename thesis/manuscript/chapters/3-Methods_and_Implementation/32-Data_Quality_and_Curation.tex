\section{Data Quality Assurance and Curation}\label{sec:data_quality_and_curation}

The multi-source corpus assembled in \Cref{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy} is heterogeneous in reliability: GBIF/iNaturalist observations carry community-contributed species labels of varying accuracy, Wikimedia Commons images are entirely unlabeled with respect to bounding boxes and may depict non-photographic subjects (illustrations, taxidermy, museum specimens), and even trusted sources occasionally contain mislabeled or low-quality frames. This section describes the staged filtering pipeline used to turn the raw corpus into a curated, quality-scored dataset with verified ground truth, and the several correction passes that were required once downstream analysis revealed problems the initial pipeline design had not anticipated.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Staged Quality Filtering Pipeline}\label{sec:staged_filtering_pipeline}

A single, uniform filtering policy across all sources was rejected in favor of a source-aware, cost-staged design. Cheap checks run first and apply to every image; expensive checks run only on the subset that survives the cheaper stages, and only where the source's reliability profile calls for them. A blanket cloud vision-language model (VLM) pass over the entire corpus was considered and rejected. Even paid API tiers cap out at approximately $4{,}000$ requests per minute, which would make processing hundreds of thousands of images impractically slow. The estimated cost for the corpus size involved was approximately $\$25$ -- small in absolute terms, but avoidable. The terms of service of most hosted VLM providers restrict large-scale automated image classification. Most importantly, a VLM pass would largely duplicate work that \textit{MegaDetector} \cite{beeryEfficientPipelineCamera2019} already performs at a fraction of the cost, by answering the simpler question of whether an animal is present at all. \Cref{fig:quality-pipeline} shows the resulting pipeline.

\paragraph{Stage 0 -- Metadata}
This stage requires no image I/O and is evaluated purely from source-provided metadata. Its pass criteria are deliberately per-source. GBIF records were to be admitted on a classifier-derived label with a score of at least $0.6$ together with a detection above confidence $0.5$ covering at least $1\%$ of the frame. iNaturalist records were admitted on carrying either a research-grade community identification or one still pending it. The latter is admitted deliberately, because a pending identification signals uncertainty about the \emph{label}, while this stage screens only for whether the file is a usable photograph -- two failure modes that should not be conflated. Wikimedia images were admitted on a shorter side of at least $320\,\mathrm{px}$, an aspect ratio no greater than $4.0$, and a photographic file type. Images from Open Images, and from \textit{LILA BC} during the later-abandoned sourcing attempt, were treated as trusted and passed through unconditionally.

The metadata criteria for the two largest sources, however, were not in fact applied. Both depend on matching an image on disk back to its source metadata record, and both links were severed by a bulk rename of the downloaded image files that was carried out for consistency of naming and that did not update the identifier mappings. The pipeline detects the total absence of matches and falls back to admitting every image on disk for those sources, emitting a warning; the fallback is guarded so that it can only trigger on a complete mismatch rather than masking a partial one. The practical consequence is that neither the research-grade filter nor the GBIF classifier-score filter contributed to the corpus, and the burden of rejecting unsuitable images for those sources fell entirely on the later stages. This is a defect rather than a design decision, and it is recorded as such; its mitigation -- that Stages 1 and 2 screen for exactly the failure modes the metadata stage would have caught, at higher cost -- is the reason it did not surface as a visible quality problem.

\paragraph{Stage 1 -- Heuristics}
Cheap, CPU-only image-level checks applied to every image regardless of source: file corruption, a minimum resolution of $256\,\mathrm{px}$ on the shorter side, a maximum aspect ratio of $4.0$, a grayscale check, and a blur check (Laplacian variance $\geq 100$ computed on a $512\,\mathrm{px}$ thumbnail). Two of these values carry a justification worth recording. The resolution floor is calibrated against the dominant source rather than chosen in the abstract: iNaturalist's standard medium-size derivative has a shorter side of roughly $281\,\mathrm{px}$, so a threshold at $320\,\mathrm{px}$ would have rejected a large share of the corpus for a property of the platform's encoding rather than of the photographs. The grayscale check is implemented as a mean saturation test in HSV space rather than as the more obvious standard deviation across channel means, because the latter statistic misfires systematically on this domain: a brown animal against brown habitat has genuinely low inter-channel variation while being a perfectly good colour photograph. The blur and aspect-ratio thresholds, by contrast, are conventional values adopted without project-specific justification.

\paragraph{Stage 2 -- MegaDetector}
\textit{MegaDetector} v5 \cite{beeryEfficientPipelineCamera2019}, a general-purpose animal/person/vehicle detector trained across a large corpus of camera-trap and non-camera-trap imagery, is run on every image surviving Stage 1. This stage is particularly effective at removing content that passes the cheaper heuristics but is not a genuine photograph of a live animal -- drawings, taxidermy mounts, plush toys, and extreme close-ups that heuristic checks alone cannot distinguish from valid images.

The detector's output is consumed under a two-threshold policy that is easy to overlook and turns out to be the most consequential single decision in the curation pipeline. Admission is strict: an image is retained only if it carries at least one animal detection above confidence $0.5$ covering at least $1\%$ of the frame. Recording is permissive: \emph{every} detection above a much lower confidence of $0.2$ is written to the image's record, whether or not it contributed to the admission decision. The two thresholds answer different questions -- \enquote{is this image worth keeping} and \enquote{what is in it} -- and separating them means that information which a single-threshold design would have discarded remains available afterwards. Every later correction in this section depends on that: the multi-box ground-truth fix of \Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}, the cross-species contamination audit, and the supplementation of the ground truth with lower-confidence boxes are all possible only because the detections were retained rather than recomputed. The alternative -- rerunning detection over the entire corpus -- would have been the single most expensive operation in the project.

\paragraph{Stage 3 -- VLM Rescue (Wikimedia Only)}
This stage is deliberately asymmetric in three ways, and each is a considered choice. It runs only on images that \emph{failed} Stage 2, since its purpose is to recover false negatives rather than to second-guess acceptances. It runs only on Wikimedia material, because that is the one source where both halves of the trade-off are large: it contains genuinely unusual real photographs that a detector may miss, and it also contains illustration and museum material, so the expected yield justifies a second expensive opinion there and nowhere else. And it defaults to acceptance: a caption produced by \textit{Florence-2-large} \cite{xiaoFlorence2AdvancingUnified2023} in detailed-captioning mode is scanned for rejection keywords (\enquote{drawing}, \enquote{illustration}, \enquote{painting}, \enquote{taxidermy}, \enquote{plush}, \enquote{cartoon}, and similar), and an image whose caption contains none of them is reinstated despite having failed detection. The risk that this admits something the detector was right to reject is knowingly deferred to the classification stage below.

\paragraph{Captioning and Caption-Based Evaluation}
As a complementary quality signal, all surviving images are captioned with \textit{Florence-2-base} \cite{xiaoFlorence2AdvancingUnified2023} (batch size $6$, $768\times768$ input, greedy decoding). The resulting captions are evaluated by a language model against three criteria: whether the subject is a real, living animal; whether the image is a genuine photograph rather than a stylized or synthetic rendering; and whether a substantial part of the animal is visible. The instruction given to the evaluator is as notable for what it forbids as for what it asks. It names, explicitly, several properties that are \emph{not} grounds for rejection -- humans present in the frame, domesticated animals, blurred or dark backgrounds, and descriptions of the animal's colour or condition. Each corresponds to a plausible failure in which a language model, asked to judge image quality from a caption, would confidently discard valid training data; the first would additionally have contradicted the deliberate inclusion of a human class. Constraining an automatic judge by enumerating its likely overreach proved more effective than refining the positive criteria. Three backend options were evaluated for this evaluation step, trading off cost against latency: a locally hosted \textit{Qwen2.5-7B-AWQ} \cite{qwenQwen25TechnicalReport2025} model served through \textit{vLLM} (an estimated $12$--$18$ hours for the full corpus), a smaller \textit{Qwen2.5-3B} model (an estimated $8$--$10$ hours), and a hosted OpenRouter endpoint (an estimated $30$--$90$ minutes at approximately $\$5$ for $543{,}000$ images). The hosted option was used for the bulk of the corpus given its favorable latency-to-cost ratio for a one-off pass.

Automatic filtering was not the end of the process. A manual review pass over the cases the automatic stages could not settle examined $17{,}737$ images, approving $13{,}827$ and declining $3{,}910$ -- a rejection rate of $22\%$, high enough to confirm that the queue was directed at genuinely ambiguous material rather than at cases the automatic stages had already resolved. This review is also what produced the stable per-class availability estimates on which the band structure of \Cref{sec:band_structure} was subsequently built; the earlier, coarser band boundaries were revised once its results were known.

\subsection{Species-Level Classification and Taxonomic Matching}\label{sec:speciesnet_matching}

Passing the quality gates above establishes that an image very likely depicts a real animal, but not which of the $225$ target classes it belongs to with sufficient confidence for training. \textit{SpeciesNet} \cite{gadotCropNotCrop2024a}, using an \textit{EfficientNetV2-M} \cite{tanEfficientNetV2SmallerModels2021} classification backbone, is run on every MegaDetector-cropped animal region to produce a taxonomic prediction. \Cref{tab:speciesnet-classification-counts} shows the number of crops classified per source; the shortfall for Open Images is a known limitation of this stage, since the vast majority of Open Images records in the corpus lacked a MegaDetector detection above threshold and were therefore never passed to the classifier.

A deliberate architectural separation governs this stage: the classifier pass stores raw output and makes no decisions, while every decision -- confidence thresholds, taxonomic tolerance, and the projection onto the $225$ classes -- is applied afterwards from that stored output. The motivation is that classification is by far the most expensive irreversible operation in the pipeline, whereas the policy applied to its results is exactly the part most likely to need revision. The correction described in \Cref{sec:speciesnet_coverage_gaps} was possible at all because revising it required re-running a filter rather than re-running inference over half a million crops.

Because the $225$-class taxonomy mixes species-, genus-, and family-level labels, a classifier prediction was accepted or rejected according to a taxonomic match-level hierarchy rather than exact label equality. Crucially, the match level is interpreted relative to the granularity of the target class rather than absolutely: a genus-level class such as \textit{Leopardus} cannot be matched at species level even by a perfect classifier, so for such a class a genus-level prediction is the finest achievable match and counts as a full one. Scoring it against a species-level bar would penalise the label schema rather than the image. A prediction one or more levels coarser than achievable counts as a partial match; a prediction that disagrees at the family level or above is treated as evidence of a mislabel.

The thresholds adopted were a minimum MegaDetector confidence of $0.5$, a minimum SpeciesNet top-1 score of $0.3$, and a family-mismatch confidence threshold of $0.5$. The first two are justified by what falls below them. Below a detection confidence of $0.5$, the crop handed to the classifier may not be a clean view of the animal at all. Below a top-1 score of $0.3$, the classifier's distribution is close to uniform over its leading candidates and carries little taxonomic information. The third rule deserves separate comment, because it inverts the usual role of confidence. For a family-level disagreement, \emph{higher} classifier confidence makes rejection \emph{more} likely: an unsure family-level prediction is most plausibly a limitation of the classifier, whereas a confident prediction asserting a different species within the family is evidence that the source label is wrong. Confidence is being read as evidence against the label rather than in support of the prediction.

A related decision concerns what the classifier is not asked. Crops below a minimum pixel size are skipped rather than classified, and the resulting record is a distinct outcome from a low-confidence prediction: the pipeline never conflates \enquote{the classifier was unsure} with \enquote{the classifier was never consulted}. In the same spirit, the projection of the classifier's distribution onto the $225$ classes retains the probability mass that falls outside them as a diagnostic rather than renormalising it away, so that an image which is genuinely out of distribution remains distinguishable from one about which the classifier is merely uncertain.

\subsection{Correcting SpeciesNet Coverage Gaps}\label{sec:speciesnet_coverage_gaps}

Applying the match-level filter of \Cref{sec:speciesnet_matching} uniformly produced an unexpected result: several classes lost the overwhelming majority of their images, for example \textit{Saguinus} species tamarins ($1{,}065 \rightarrow 5$ images), \textit{Eulemur} species lemurs ($880 \rightarrow 2$), rock hyrax ($1{,}336 \rightarrow 7$), \textit{Ateles} species spider monkeys ($1{,}437 \rightarrow 38$), and pikas ($1{,}484 \rightarrow 31$) -- shrinkages of $97\%$ to $99\%$. Investigating these cases individually showed that the drop was not evidence of widespread mislabeling in the source data. Instead, it reflected a coverage gap in \textit{SpeciesNet} itself: the model simply does not reliably recognize several of these taxa. It instead emits low-confidence or overly coarse predictions (\texttt{match\_level\_no\_match} or \texttt{match\_level\_class}) for images that a visual inspection confirmed were correctly labeled at the source.

Treating a low pass rate under the match-level filter as evidence of a classifier coverage gap, rather than a labeling problem, required a fallback rule. If a class's dominant failure reason is \texttt{match\_level\_no\_match} or \texttt{match\_level\_class} and fewer than $20\%$ of its images pass the filter (or the dominant failure reason is \texttt{match\_level\_order} and fewer than $15\%$ pass), the class falls back to using the pre-classification quality-pass flag directly. This bypasses the SpeciesNet-based filter entirely. Applying this fallback recovered approximately $14{,}500$ images across $17$ affected classes. Two candidate classes -- Asian elephant and domestic water buffalo -- were deliberately kept under the stricter filter despite low pass rates. Their dominant failure reason was \texttt{family\_mismatch\_high\_confidence}, a genuine signal of label contamination rather than a coverage gap: the classifier was confidently predicting a \emph{different} family, not merely failing to commit to a species.

A related, smaller-scale problem was traced to inconsistent handling of apostrophes in class folder names (for example \enquote{grevys zebra} versus the canonical \enquote{grevy's zebra}). This had silently created duplicate \enquote{ghost} classes and left approximately $59$ images unreviewed and uncounted. The fix normalizes apostrophes through a canonical-name lookup applied consistently across the classification and filtering scripts. Two further species -- sea otter ($1{,}404$ images) and Hoffmann's two-toed sloth ($667$ images) -- showed a $100\%$ SpeciesNet failure rate under \texttt{match\_level\_no\_match}; rather than force a fallback threshold for these two cases specifically, SpeciesNet filtering was skipped entirely for them, and all images that passed the MegaDetector and caption-filter stages were retained.

\subsection{Ground-Truth Annotation Integrity}\label{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}

\paragraph{The Multi-Box Annotation Bug}
A later audit of the assembled test annotations discovered that every one of $63{,}796$ images in the test annotation file carried exactly one ground-truth bounding box. % annotations_test.json
MegaDetector, however, had detected multiple animals in a substantial fraction of the underlying images. The root cause was a data-loading function that silently dropped the full \texttt{detections} array before it reached the COCO-annotation builder, so only a single detection per image could ever be emitted regardless of how many animals MegaDetector had actually found. The scale of the resulting gap varied by source: approximately $26\%$ of GBIF images, $15\%$ of iNaturalist images, $26\%$ of Wikimedia images, and $31\%$ of Open Images images (at confidence $\geq 0.5$) contained more than one detection that had been silently discarded.

The fix forwards the full detections array through the pipeline and emits one COCO annotation per detection above a confidence threshold of $0.5$, for every image. Applying the fix also required removing $43$ Wikimedia images that had zero detections after the correction (while explicitly retaining $26$ genuinely blank/background images that had been correctly included as negatives). After the fix, the splits comprised $145{,}764$ training images ($178{,}924$ annotations, up to $28$ boxes per image), $12{,}545$ validation images ($18{,}445$ annotations, up to $22$ boxes per image), and $63{,}822$ test images ($86{,}785$ annotations, up to $28$ boxes per image). These are intermediate figures: the contamination review and the low-confidence supplementation described below changed the annotation counts again, and the final composition is given in \Cref{sec:final_dataset_composition}.

\paragraph{Cross-Species Contamination in Multi-Box Images}
Fixing the multi-box bug exposed a second, more subtle defect: every bounding box on a given image had been assigned the same category label as the image's own primary classification, so a second animal of a \emph{different} species appearing in the same photograph would be mislabeled as the primary species. Rather than manually reviewing all such images, the existing per-box SpeciesNet classification (\Cref{sec:speciesnet_matching}) was reused to flag a secondary box automatically. A box was flagged only when its predicted taxon diverged from the image's primary label by more than a taxonomic tolerance band. Predictions consistent at species, genus, or family level were treated as acceptable variation -- for example, different zebra species, or different elephant species within one genus. Divergence at order level, class level, or a complete non-match was flagged instead, subject to a minimum SpeciesNet confidence of $0.3$ on the secondary box. This taxonomic-tolerance design was necessary because a naive flag on any label disagreement would have queued an unmanageable number of images for manual review. Of $57{,}205$ images with at least two confident detections, $32{,}401$ ($56.6\%$) had a differing SpeciesNet species index on a secondary box. The great majority of these, however, were genus- or family-level siblings rather than genuine contamination.

Two assumptions bound what this audit can find, and both should be stated. First, the highest-confidence detection on an image is taken to be correct by construction -- it is, after all, the box that determined the image's own label -- and only secondary boxes are checked, so an image whose \emph{primary} box is the contaminant is never flagged. Second, a secondary box whose prediction differs but whose confidence is low is routed to a separate uncertain list rather than flagged, which deliberately trades recall of contamination for a review queue that a single person can actually work through. The binding constraint at this stage was human review time, not detection sensitivity, and the design reflects that rather than pretending to exhaustiveness.

The taxonomic-tolerance filter flagged $11{,}354$ images for manual review. Of these, $7{,}271$ entries were ultimately reviewed and resolved as $6{,}477$ \enquote{keep} (the secondary box was confirmed correctly labeled), $794$ \enquote{edit} (the secondary box's label was corrected), and zero \enquote{discard}. Consequently, no images were removed from the dataset, and only $1{,}344$ mislabeled annotations were deleted across the three splits ($785$ from training, $100$ from validation, $459$ from test).

A follow-on pass supplemented the ground truth with a further $16{,}911$ boxes drawn from the $10$--$50\%$ detection-confidence range, on the reasoning that the two-threshold recording policy of \Cref{sec:staged_filtering_pipeline} had preserved exactly these detections for a purpose such as this one. Two properties of that pass matter. Its ordering after the contamination removal is a correctness requirement rather than a convenience: running it first would have re-introduced boxes that the review had deliberately deleted. And the boxes it adds are de-duplicated by overlap not only against existing annotations but against boxes accepted earlier in the same pass, which is what makes it safe to re-run. Its significant limitation is that these boxes were added with no species verification at all -- the taxonomic guard for this pass was specified and left unimplemented -- which is precisely why a second flagging-and-removal pass exists that examines only this class of box, evaluating $21{,}026$ of them and flagging $1{,}929$. The supplementation is therefore better described as a two-step procedure in which the second step repairs the first than as a single clean addition.

As a byproduct, the review produced empirical evidence about which species are actually confused with which. Where the classifier assigned a different species to a region of the same animal or its surroundings, its error identifies a confusion partner for that class. Mining the $6{,}477$ confirmed-correct cases on this basis yielded $1{,}733$ candidate pairs, subject to a minimum-evidence floor so that a class with only one or two such rejections contributes no signal.

These candidates feed the frozen look-alike grouping used for coarse-grained evaluation (\Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}), whose governing criterion is worth stating here because it is a judgement about what counts as a failure rather than a technical rule. Two classes are grouped if confusing them \emph{in a single still frame} is a forgivable error that should be charged to fine-grained discrimination, and kept apart if confusing them is a genuine failure that must surface at the coarse level. The consequences are deliberately uneven: leopard and jaguar are merged, since telling them apart from one still image is a specialist task, while lion and tiger are forced into separate single-member groups despite sharing a genus, because confusing them is not a forgivable error under any reading. When a case is uncertain, the default is not to merge. The resulting table is frozen once evaluation begins -- any later change would silently invalidate previously computed coarse-level scores -- and the earlier, purely taxonomic version is retained as an audit artefact rather than overwritten.

\subsection{Quality Scoring and Split Assembly}\label{sec:quality_scoring_splits}

Images surviving all filtering stages were assigned a composite quality score $Q \in [0, 1]$ used to guide validation-set sampling and to break ties during split assembly:
\begin{equation}
    Q = 0.30\,S_{\text{area}} + 0.25\,S_{\text{edge}} + 0.20\,S_{\text{single}} + 0.25\,S_{\text{conf}}.
    \label{eq:quality-score}
\end{equation}
$S_{\text{area}}$ rewards a bounding box occupying between $4\%$ and $40\%$ of the frame (a score of $1.0$), decaying toward $0$ below $2\%$ or above $70\%$ of frame area. $S_{\text{edge}}$ penalizes boxes that are clipped by the image boundary, falling to $0$ once the box extends outside the frame. $S_{\text{single}}$ rewards images with exactly one detection ($1.00$) over zero detections ($0.50$), two detections ($0.60$), or three or more ($0.30$), reflecting that single-animal images are generally easier to verify and less prone to the contamination problem of \Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}. And $S_{\text{conf}}$ is the raw MegaDetector bounding-box confidence (defaulting to $0.30$ when unavailable). Two hard exclusions apply regardless of the weighted score: an object occupying less than $1\%$ of the frame, or a bounding box that extends beyond the image boundary, both force $Q = 0$.

Before any allocation, one class of image is removed entirely: an image for which the detector found no animal at all is dropped from the splits rather than retained as a hard example. The argument is about the evaluation metric rather than about data quality. Such an image carries no ground-truth box, so it can never yield a true positive or a false negative -- any prediction on it scores as a false positive and a missed animal is invisible. Including such images would therefore depress precision systematically and asymmetrically, measuring the dataset's annotation coverage rather than the detector's behaviour. Deliberate true negatives are a different matter and are handled separately below.

The three splits are then drawn under deliberately different rules, because they answer different questions. The test set is drawn first, and stratified so that its mixture of sources mirrors the class's actual pool: a test set is an estimate of behaviour on the operating distribution and must not be a curated subset of it. Training images are then taken greedily by quality score from what remains, which is a legitimate bias -- there is no reason to train on the worst available images when better ones exist. Validation, finally, is sampled from the $30$th to $70$th percentile of $Q$ within each class under a fixed seed. The reasoning here follows from the training rule rather than standing alone: because training has been skimmed from the top of the quality distribution, a uniformly random validation set would be systematically easier than the data the model will face, and early stopping would fire on an optimistic signal. Taking the middle band gives a validation set that is representative rather than either best or worst case, and the generated split report audits the outcome by tabulating validation mean quality against the pool mean for each band.

For the largest classes (Band D, see \Cref{sec:synthetic_data_supplementation}), training-image selection additionally applied a source-diversity cap. This limited any single source to at most $60\%$ of a class's training images when at least three sources were available ($75\%$ with two sources, no cap with only one). The purpose was to prevent a single source's photographic style from becoming the feature the model learns for a well-populated class. The cap tightens as alternatives become available, and -- importantly -- it is soft: if a class cannot fill its training quota under the cap, the cap is relaxed rather than the quota reduced. Filling the training budget takes priority over source diversity, since a hard cap would have quietly shrunk the training sets of exactly those classes whose imagery comes predominantly from one place.

Two further details make the split reproducible in practice rather than only in principle. Every sampling step is preceded by a canonical sort whose final key is the file path, so that images with identical quality scores cannot be ordered arbitrarily between runs -- without which a fixed seed guarantees nothing. And the background negatives are shuffled under an offset seed, so their assignment is uncorrelated with that of the human class rather than sharing a random stream with it.

The deliberate negatives sit outside the band logic altogether. The $174$ background photographs are split in fixed proportions, because band logic is a function of a class's pool size and that quantity is meaningless for images belonging to no class. The human images likewise bypass quality scoring, since they were selected upstream by criteria -- subject size band, distance from the frame edge, upright aspect -- that are essentially the same geometric conditions the quality score measures. They were additionally excluded outright if any animal or vehicle appeared in the frame, so that the negative class can never double as an unlabelled animal positive.

In the exported annotation files this corresponds to $145{,}728$ training images carrying $187{,}705$ boxes, $12{,}543$ validation images carrying $19{,}732$ boxes, and $63{,}802$ test images carrying $92{,}094$ boxes -- $222{,}073$ real images and $299{,}531$ annotations in total, across $225$ categories. Every annotation records its own provenance, distinguishing boxes produced by the detector above the primary threshold, boxes recovered from the lower-confidence range, and boxes supplied with the human negatives, so that the machine-versus-human origin of any published box remains auditable. The real images carry no hand-drawn ground truth at all: every box originates from automatic detection, and human effort went into verifying and correcting labels rather than into drawing boxes.

Two structural properties of this table are worth noting. Band A contributes no training images by design -- its classes are supported entirely by synthetic imagery (\Cref{sec:band_structure}) -- which is why its row is zero rather than small. And Band D carries $231{,}107$ unallocated surplus images: these are not discarded but simply exceed the per-class caps, and they are what made the enlarged test set of the generator comparison (\Cref{sec:generator_comparison_classes}) possible without touching any image the main experiment trains on.

\paragraph{Known Defects in the Split}
Three defects were identified in a review of the assembled split and are recorded here rather than left to be discovered downstream. First, $99$ hard-excluded images -- those whose bounding box extends past the frame boundary and which therefore score $Q = 0$ -- reached the Band A test set. This follows directly from the Band A rule described above: because those classes assign their entire pool to testing without quality filtering, the hard-exclusion step never runs for them. The reasoning that produced the defect is defensible in itself (a truncated animal is still worth having when a class has fewer than $150$ images) but its interaction with the exclusion rule was not intended. Second, band assignment was computed from a conservative estimate of each class's pool: the classifier-vetted count. The exported dataset, however, contains every quality-passed image, so some classes hold more imagery than their band implies. A number of non-Band-D classes therefore sit one band lower than their actual pool would place them. This is documented as expected behaviour of the construction strategy rather than corrected, and the discrepancy is preserved in the reports for auditability. Third, the Band B and C test sets are quality-biased low by construction, since training takes the top of the quality distribution and validation the middle, leaving the remainder to test. The effect is bounded but real. It means absolute accuracy on those bands is not directly comparable with Band D's.

\paragraph{A Note on Tuning}
The quality-score weights and breakpoints, the band boundaries, the per-band training and test quotas, the $30$th-to-$70$th percentile validation window, the blur threshold and the overlap threshold used for box de-duplication are all fixed design choices, justified by reasoning of the kind given above but not selected by any sensitivity analysis. No ablation over these values was performed, and the dataset was not re-assembled under alternative settings to measure how much they matter. This is stated explicitly because the alternative -- presenting them without comment -- would imply a tuning process that did not take place.
