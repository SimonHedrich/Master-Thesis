\section{Synthetic Data Supplementation}\label{sec:synthetic_data_supplementation}

Even after the sourcing and curation effort described in \Cref{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy,sec:data_quality_and_curation}, real-image availability remains highly skewed across the $225$-class taxonomy: some classes are supported by thousands of curated images, while others have only a handful. Since a knowledge-distillation-versus-direct-fine-tuning comparison is only meaningful if every class receives a comparable and sufficient training signal, classes with a small real-image pool were supplemented with synthetically generated imagery. This section describes the band structure used to decide which classes receive synthetic supplementation and how much, the image-generation pipeline itself, two diversity-related corrections made after inspecting early output, the bounding-box annotation procedure for synthetic images, and the construction of a separate, balanced synthetic test set used as a secondary evaluation instrument.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Band Structure: Matching Supplementation Strategy to Data Availability}\label{sec:band_structure}

Classes were grouped into four bands according to the size of their real-image pool after curation, with each band receiving a different combination of real and synthetic imagery for training and a correspondingly different amount of real imagery reserved for testing. \Cref{tab:band-structure} defines the bands. The boundaries (real-image pool sizes of $150$, $250$, and $400$) were arrived at only after the full curation pipeline had run and a substantial manual review pass ($17{,}737$ images reviewed, $13{,}827$ approved) had produced a stable estimate of per-class real-image availability; an earlier draft of this strategy had used coarser tiers at $100$, $500$, and $1{,}500$ images, which were revised once the actual post-curation distribution was known.

The equal $200$-image training budget shared by Bands A, B and C is the point of the structure rather than a convenience. It makes three training regimes -- synthetic only, half real and half synthetic, and real only -- comparable at matched sample size, which is the dataset's own contribution to the thesis's question about synthetic data. One caveat must be stated alongside it, because it bounds what the comparison can support: the three bands contain \emph{different species}, assigned by data availability rather than by randomisation. This is a cross-band comparison, not a controlled within-class experiment, and any systematic difference in how difficult the species in one band are to detect is confounded with the training regime applied to it. The optional Band D extension described below was designed precisely to provide the within-class comparison that the band structure cannot.

A further principle runs through every band: surplus real images beyond a training budget are never discarded to enforce a cap, but moved to the test set instead. This is why Band D test sizes vary rather than being fixed, and why the corpus carries a large unallocated surplus (\Cref{sec:final_dataset_composition}) rather than a truncated pool.

Band A comprises the $50$ most data-poor classes (for example walrus, old world porcupine family, raccoon dog, kinkajou, water deer, Eurasian badger, and nine-banded armadillo), whose training set is entirely synthetic; their real images, averaging approximately $67$ per class, are reserved in full for testing, though roughly $17$ of these $50$ classes have fewer than $30$ real test images and are consequently flagged as test-limited by definition rather than by any model deficiency. Band B ($26$ classes, including Canada lynx, spectacled bear, caracal, giant panda, chimpanzee, and springbok) supplements a $100$-image real training set with $100$ synthetic images to reach the same $200$-image training budget as Band A. Band C ($26$ classes, including muntjac genus, red panda, snow leopard, and dingo) reaches the $200$-image budget entirely from real imagery and receives no synthetic supplementation, serving as the real-data control against the synthetic-heavy Bands A and B at an equal training-set size. Band D ($122$ classes) covers the data-rich end of the taxonomy, from japanese macaque ($420$ pool images, $336$ train / $84$ test) up to squirrel family ($31{,}159$ pool images, capped at $1{,}500$ train / $500$ test); the two smallest classes with zero real images at all, \texttt{human} and \texttt{unmatched}, are excluded from every band.


The synthetic-image budget follows directly from the band assignment: Band A requires $50$ classes $\times\ 200$ images $= 10{,}000$ synthetic images (an estimated \texteuro$500$ at the per-image generation cost used), and Band B requires a further $26$ classes $\times\ 100$ images $= 2{,}600$ (\texteuro$130$), for a combined total of $12{,}600$ synthetic training images at approximately \texteuro$630$, comfortably within an \texteuro$800$ budget cap with an \texteuro$170$ buffer. An optional extension, not part of the primary experimental plan, was documented for five Band D classes (cheetah, tiger, gorilla, sea otter, and mountain goat): training each under a synthetic-only $200$-image condition and a real-only $200$-image condition on an identical held-out test set, to obtain a direct apples-to-apples comparison of synthetic and real training data at matched sample size, at an additional estimated cost of \texteuro$50$.

\paragraph{An Implementation Gap in the Band A Design}
The band structure allocates Band A's training signal entirely to synthetic imagery, and that allocation is the premise of every long-tail claim this thesis makes. For the first generation of trained models it did not hold. All three training pipelines read only the real-image annotation files, which by construction contain no Band A images at all, and no code path ever loaded the synthetic training annotations. Every model trained before this was discovered therefore had literally no exposure to the $50$ Band A species, and their Band A scores measure zero-shot behaviour rather than weak supervision -- which is the correct reading of the exactly-zero Band A accuracy that first prompted the investigation. The data loaders were corrected to merge the two annotation sources (giving $155{,}808$ training and $15{,}063$ validation images), and the affected models were retrained; \Cref{chapter:results} reports post-correction figures throughout. The episode is recorded here because a defect of this kind is invisible in the dataset itself -- the synthetic images existed, were correctly annotated, and were simply never read.

\subsection{Synthetic Image Generation Pipeline}\label{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}

Synthetic images were produced with \textit{Gemini}'s \texttt{gemini-3.1-flash-image-preview} image-generation model \cite{google_gemini_2024}. The choice of generator was driven primarily by prompt-length capacity: \textit{Gemini} accepts prompts of up to $32{,}000$ tokens, compared to roughly $480$ tokens for \textit{Imagen}, $6{,}000$ characters for \textit{Midjourney}, $256$ tokens for \textit{DALL-E}, and a hard $77$-token CLIP limit for \textit{Stable Diffusion}. Since the prompt construction strategy below relies on inserting substantial verbatim reference text per image, only a generator with a sufficiently large prompt budget was viable. Beyond that constraint, the model was selected by inspecting sample outputs -- a basis that is insufficient for a decision of this weight, and that is revisited under a controlled protocol in \Cref{sec:generator_comparison}. The present section describes the pipeline as it was built and run; whether the generator at its centre was the right one is a question that section answers.

Why the prompts must be species-specific at all is worth establishing before describing them. A generic instruction to depict an animal in its habitat fails here for three reasons that compound: behaviour is species-specific, so a cross-product of generic scenes produces a walrus climbing a tree or a kinkajou on an ice floe, and a training image that asserts an impossible association is worse than no image at all; species discrimination depends on markers that a generic prompt never demands, so the model is free to render a plausible generic mammal that carries none of them; and habitat distributions differ so widely that generic backgrounds actively misrepresent where a species occurs, degrading rather than improving robustness.

\paragraph{Six Axes of Variation}
Each generated image is specified along six largely independent axes to avoid the repetitive, canonical-pose imagery that a single fixed prompt template per species would otherwise produce: camera angle and perspective (eye-level, low, high, side-profile, three-quarter front or rear, head-on, and rear views); subject distance, calibrated against a simulated $400$--$600\,\mathrm{mm}$ telephoto lens across three bands (close, $20$--$80\,\mathrm{m}$; medium, $80$--$250\,\mathrm{m}$; far, beyond $250\,\mathrm{m}$); pose and behavior, drawn from a universal pool supplemented with species-specific behaviors; environment and habitat, covering ten codes including \texttt{in\_water}, \texttt{canopy}, and \texttt{burrow}; lighting, covering five codes; and occlusion or visibility, where partial vegetation occlusion is capped at approximately $10\%$ of images per class and semi-submerged framing is mandatory for aquatic or semi-aquatic species. An eight-guild ecological system (arboreal, semi-aquatic, fully aquatic, fossorial, cold-climate, arid/savanna, large-grazing, and primate) further biases the environment and behavior axes toward ecologically plausible combinations for each species. In practice the guilds act less directly than their prominence suggests: the primary channel is a set of generic guild rules stated in the scene-generation instruction, which asks the language model to determine which apply to the species in front of it, while the per-species guild assignment serves mainly to select a fallback habitat when scene generation fails. For species assigned to more than one guild, only the first is used on that fallback path.

Two further axes were specified in the design and not implemented, and this should be recorded rather than passed over: target proportions of roughly $15$--$20\%$ juvenile subjects, and approximately even sex representation for strongly dimorphic species. Neither age nor sex is conditioned anywhere in the generation pipeline, so whatever variation the dataset exhibits along those axes is incidental to the language model's scene descriptions rather than controlled. Since juvenile animals and the less-ornamented sex are exactly the cases a detector is most likely to miss, this is a substantive gap in the synthetic data's coverage rather than a cosmetic one.

\paragraph{Prompt Construction}
Prompts are assembled from a fixed template with five blocks -- subject, species description, scene specification, photography style, and critical requirements -- into which the raw text of a species' Wikipedia Description, Behaviour, and Habitat sections is inserted verbatim rather than summarized, on the reasoning that summarization risks discarding visually relevant detail (fur pattern, characteristic posture) that a generative model can otherwise make use of directly.

The template's constraints are best understood as a catalogue of defences, each aimed at a failure mode observed in practice. The requirement that the animal be fully visible with no part cut off at the frame edge exists because a subject already cropped at the boundary cannot be plausibly scaled down by the training-time scale augmentation of \Cref{sec:data_augmentation} -- there is no image content to reveal as it shrinks. An explicit instruction not to reorient the animal toward the camera in order to make its features more visible counteracts a strong and persistent tendency of generative models to produce forward-facing subjects, which would collapse the camera-angle axis precisely where it is most valuable. A requirement of exactly one individual of the target species, with other species permitted incidentally in the background, keeps automatic single-box annotation valid without sterilising the scene. And the prohibition on text, watermarks, borders and anachronistic human-made objects removes artefacts that a detector would otherwise be free to treat as evidence.

The style constraints work by substitution rather than negation, which turns out to matter. Rather than only forbidding the unwanted look, the prompt supplies a competing one: documentary wildlife photography of a named editorial standard, under authentic field conditions, viewed through optical binoculars. Negations are retained as a backstop, but the positive anchor does most of the work -- an instruction to avoid illustration is far weaker than an instruction to produce a particular kind of photograph. For genus- or family-level classes, the top five most-observed constituent species (by iNaturalist observation count) are used as representative stand-ins, excluding any species that is already covered as a standalone class elsewhere in the taxonomy.

\paragraph{Generation Schedule and Curated Validation Set}
Band A classes receive $200$ images each, split into $160$ training images and $40$ validation images. Critically, the $40$ validation images are not drawn at random from the same generation distribution as the training images: they use a fixed set of \enquote{prototypical} shot parameters (eye-level or three-quarter-front angle, medium distance, standing-alert or similar canonical poses, overcast or golden-hour lighting, minimal occlusion) deliberately chosen to resemble typical field photography. A randomly sampled validation set would optimize development decisions for synthetic-image performance specifically; the curated set instead functions as a proxy for real-image performance, which is the quantity ultimately of interest. Band B classes receive $100$ images per class, of which $80$ are allocated to training and $20$ to validation under the same prototypical criteria; this allocation was applied retroactively, the initial design having assigned all $100$ to training.

\paragraph{Photography Style Correction}
An early design iteration used a shallow-depth-of-field, \enquote{bokeh} telephoto-portrait style intended to mimic professional wildlife photography. Comparison against the $10$ genuine \textit{AX Visio} captures available (\texttt{resources/AX\_Visio\_images/}) showed that the binocular's optics produce no background blur at any distance: the animal is embedded in a sharp scene rather than isolated against a softened one. Training on bokeh-style imagery would therefore have supplied a subject-background separation cue that is simply absent at inference, which is a self-inflicted domain gap of exactly the kind the synthetic pipeline exists to avoid. The evidence base for this correction is small and should be read as such, but the property it establishes is a categorical one about the optics rather than a statistical one about scenes. The style was corrected by substituting the competing anchor described above -- observation through binoculars, full-scene sharp focus -- with the explicit negation of background blur kept only as a backstop. The $380$ images already generated under the bokeh style at the time of this correction were retained in the dataset but are explicitly flagged as a non-target style rather than deleted outright; the remaining $12{,}220$ images were generated under the corrected style.

A related, smaller correction removed a \texttt{closeup\_head} camera-angle option that had originally been included alongside the eye-level and medium-distance options. Close, head-cropped framing was found to conflict with a later training-time augmentation step (random scale reduction, \Cref{sec:data_augmentation}): an animal whose full body is visible in the frame can be scaled down plausibly, whereas one already cropped at the image edge cannot be, since there is no image content to reveal as it shrinks. The option was replaced with additional eye-level, medium-distance shots instead.

\paragraph{Format, Content Safety, and Quality Control}
Images were requested at the generator's smallest offered size in a $4{:}3$ aspect ratio, and are written at $592 \times 448$ pixels. Both choices are economic rather than aesthetic. The resolution is a cost floor: synthetic images are letterboxed down to the detector's $320$--$416\,\mathrm{px}$ input during training in any case, so the smallest tier that still preserves diagnostic detail is the one that maximises images per unit of budget, and paying for a larger tier would buy detail the training pipeline immediately discards. The aspect ratio minimises the padding overhead of that letterboxing -- approximately $6\%$ wasted area, against approximately $44\%$ for a $16{:}9$ frame resized into a square input tensor -- while still matching the framing conventions of real field photography and leaving distant subjects large enough to remain detectable, which a wider frame would not.

Content safety was handled by rewriting prompts rather than by configuring the generator, since the API surfaces a refusal as an empty response indistinguishable from a transient failure. Prompt construction avoids graphic predation, explicit mating behaviour and cruelty-adjacent scenes from the outset, and where generated output proved defective the responsible prompts were revised and the images regenerated: $10$ images were quarantined and four prompts rewritten, every one of them involving carrion or feeding on prey. Three of the four substituted an alert or stationary pose for a foraging one, which has a side effect worth recording -- the scavenging species' behaviour distribution was shifted away from feeding, against the intent of the diversity constraints described below.

Every generated image is subsequently passed through \textit{MegaDetector} \cite{beery_megadetector_2019} as a quality check; images without a qualifying detection are flagged for regeneration. An expected detection rate above $90\%$ was assumed when sizing the generation budget, leaving headroom of approximately $10\%$ regeneration within the \texteuro$170$ buffer described in \Cref{sec:band_structure}.

\subsection{Prompt Diversity Refinement}\label{sec:prompt_diversity}

An early design question was how to vary habitat, viewpoint, lighting, and behavior across the images generated for a single species without producing ecologically implausible combinations. A generic cross-product of these axes (habitat $\times$ viewpoint $\times$ lighting) was considered and rejected, since it readily produces nonsensical scenes -- an aye-aye grazing in open savanna at noon, or a pangolin swimming in an alpine lake. The approach adopted instead pre-generates, once per species, a bank of plausible ecological scenarios using a lower-cost language model, varying behavior, substrate, viewpoint, lighting, and season jointly and coherently rather than independently.

A manual review of the first $50$ generated images per class nonetheless revealed a diversity problem in the resulting imagery: walking and standing-alert poses dominated, animals were shown facing the camera far more often than a genuine field photograph would suggest, and several near-identical image pairs appeared, traced to a fixed set of five scenario prompts cycling deterministically across generation batches. The cause had two parts. The scenario-generation instruction did not explicitly require variation along these axes, so the language model producing the scene bank converged on the modal description of an animal; and environments are drawn from a fixed per-species bank of eight by deterministic cycling, so a class whose bank was narrow produced visibly repeated backgrounds. This is why the remedy enriches and constrains the bank rather than resampling from it. The fix added explicit diversity constraints to the scenario-generation step: at least one resting, lying, or sleeping behavior and at least one active foraging behavior per class; at least one non-frontal-gaze behavior; no more than one walking or standing-alert pose per generation batch; guild-specific minimums (for example, at least one climbing behavior for arboreal-guild species, at least one digging behavior for fossorial-guild species); and an explicit instruction forbidding the model from reorienting the animal to face the camera regardless of the specified viewpoint. Affected classes were regenerated by clearing the cached scenario profiles and deleting pending prompt files for images not yet generated, leaving already-generated images untouched.

\subsection{Bounding-Box Annotation of Synthetic Images}\label{sec:synthetic_annotation}

Synthetic images require the same bounding-box ground truth as real images before they can be used for detector training. The routing rule that organises this work follows directly from the single-animal constraint written into the prompts: an image on which the detector finds exactly one animal is the case the prompt intended, and needs only cheap confirmation, whereas an image with none or with two or more is one where either the constraint or the detector failed, and needs a box drawn by hand. Concentrating manual effort on the cases where something demonstrably went wrong is what made annotating $12{,}600$ images tractable at all. Applying this to the generation batch produced $2$ images ($0.0\%$) with no significant detection, $12{,}445$ ($98.8\%$) with exactly one, and $153$ ($1.2\%$) with two or more; $403$ images were ultimately labelled by hand.

Three properties of this procedure qualify how far the resulting ground truth can be trusted, and none is visible from the distribution above. First, the confirmation step is a negative-confirmation interface: the reviewer clicks only to \emph{reject} the detector's single-animal reading, and every image left unclicked is committed as a positive confirmation. The step therefore measures the reviewer's rejection rate rather than an independent judgement on each image, and reviewer inattention biases strictly toward agreeing with the detector. The $98.8\%$ figure should be read as an upper bound on verification rather than as a count of confirmations. Second, the synthetic test images bypass this confirmation entirely: a single detection is accepted as ground truth without human review, on the reasoning that the test set's images are generated under the most canonical shot parameters and are least likely to be ambiguous. Third, boxes are clamped to the image boundary on export, which silently absorbs precisely the truncated-subject failure mode the prompt requirements were written to prevent -- a truncated animal yields a valid-looking box rather than a detectable error. Against these, the procedure records the provenance of every box, distinguishing detector output from human-drawn boxes and preserving that distinction when a detector box is subsequently adjusted, and its review decisions are append-only with replayable undo rather than edited in place.

\subsection{The Balanced Synthetic Test Set}\label{sec:synthetic_test_set}

Because the real test set varies enormously in size across bands (from as few as $6$ real images for the most data-poor Band A classes up to $500$ for the largest Band D classes), the real test set alone cannot support certain cross-class or cross-band comparisons without the comparison itself being confounded by differing test-set sizes. \Cref{tab:synthetic-test-motivation} summarizes the specific problems this creates and the standardized $225 \times 50$ synthetic test set constructed to address them.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Motivation for a fixed-size, per-class synthetic test set.}
\label{tab:synthetic-test-motivation}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}Xl@{}}
\toprule
Problem & Mitigation \\
\midrule
Real test-set size varies from $6$ to $500$ images per class & Fixed $50$ images per class regardless of real availability \\
Band D classes have no standardized evaluation probe & A common $50$-image probe applies uniformly across bands \\
Cross-band accuracy comparisons are confounded by test-set-size differences & Equal $50$-image weight per class \\
Condition-specific failure analysis is impossible on uncontrolled real images & Known, fixed angle/behavior/lighting distribution enables structured ablations \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The origin of this test set is partly budgetary and is stated plainly: the production run did not use the generator's half-price batch interface, and the remaining self-funded budget was spent on a standardised evaluation probe rather than on additional training images. That it was worth spending on is the argument of \Cref{tab:synthetic-test-motivation}; that it existed to be spent is an accident of billing.

The synthetic test images follow the same \enquote{prototypical} shot design as the Band A curated validation set (\Cref{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}): a fixed distribution of ten images each across standing-alert, walking, eating/foraging, resting, and looking-at-camera behaviors, all at eye-level or three-quarter-front angle and medium distance, under overcast or golden-hour lighting, with minimal occlusion, rendered in the corrected binocular-observation style. An optional sixth drinking-behavior category was added for some classes to reach an even $225 \times 56 = 12{,}600$ total. This test set is explicitly a secondary, diagnostic instrument rather than a replacement for real-image evaluation: it is used in combination with the real test set as described in the mixed evaluation methodology of \Cref{sec:evaluation_framework}, never in isolation, since a model must never be judged on synthetic imagery alone. The final, verified synthetic test set comprises $225$ categories, $11{,}250$ images, and $11{,}264$ bounding boxes, with category identifiers matching the real test set exactly so the two can be merged directly for the mixed evaluation condition. The optional sixth behaviour was not generated, so the set is exactly $50$ images per class rather than $56$. Its behavioural composition is exactly balanced by construction -- $2{,}250$ images in each of the five behaviours -- which is what makes condition-level comparison across classes meaningful.

One design asymmetry between this set and the training images should be noted. The behaviour descriptions used for training are phrased terrestrially, so an arboreal or aquatic species is asked to walk with a natural gait; the test set's descriptions were deliberately rewritten to be species-adaptive, allowing climbing, swimming or wading as appropriate. The intention was to avoid demanding implausible postures of non-terrestrial species in evaluation, but the consequence is that those species are described differently in training than in testing -- a small self-inflicted domain shift affecting exactly the guilds whose imagery is scarcest.
