\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{chapter:discussion_conclusion}

This chapter addresses the four research questions based on the evidence from \Cref{chapter:results}. \Cref{sec:limitations} outlines the limitations of these answers, \Cref{sec:further_work} describes the subsequent work, and \Cref{sec:conclusion} summarizes the thesis's contributions.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The four research questions of \Cref{sec:objective} are repeated below. Each answer describes what was learned rather than presenting the measurements themselves, which are found in the referenced tables. All statements about accuracy refer to the real test set.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does knowledge distillation from a fine-tuned teacher model into a nano-scale student model yield higher detection accuracy than fine-tuning the same student directly on the wildlife dataset, given the domain shift from COCO-style classes to $225$ mammal species?
    \item Which synthetic-image generator produces the most useful training data for this domain, and do the two criteria available before any detector is trained, apparent visual realism and per-image price, predict that ranking?
    \item How does the composition of a class's training data, from synthetic images alone through a large pool of real photographs, affect detection accuracy, and does accuracy measured on the mixed test set hold up on the real test set alone?
    \item How far is a nano-scale detector from real-time inference without quantization or any other compression step, and how much latency and model size does it save against the two-stage ensembles, measured on a target-adjacent proxy device?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Question $1$: Distillation Against Direct Fine-Tuning}
Direct fine-tuning is a better approach for this student. The directly trained model is clearly more accurate than the distilled model in every test domain, at every level of granularity, and in every band (\Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}). The gap is widest in Band A, where the training images are synthetic and scarce, and where the teacher's soft targets were expected to be the most helpful. However, the teacher's class distribution had no apparent benefit beyond what the labels already contained. Where it deviated from the labels, it even seems to have reduced the student's accuracy. \Cref{sec:limitations} explains why this form of distillation could contribute so little. Only one distillation configuration and one teacher-student pairing were tested, and the two runs differed in batch size (\Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}). The result rules out any benefit from this configuration. However, it does not rule out knowledge distillation for this domain.

\paragraph{Question $2$: Generator Choice}
The most useful training images came from a proprietary API service at its lowest quality level and were prompted with the full, unabridged description (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Neither of the two criteria that could be evaluated beforehand predicted this outcome. The generator that was chosen for the production dataset because its images appeared the most realistic ranked in the upper middle of the field, clearly behind the leader when using the same prompt. Price did not predict usefulness either. In no vendor family was the cheaper tier worse than the expensive one. The outcome is predicted by how much detail the generator receives. Shortening the prompt roughly halved the accuracy of both tested models downstream, a larger effect than most differences between generators (\Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}). Earlier work found the same. Bare class-name prompts yield poor training data, and alignment with the target classes is more important than visual fidelity \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. This ranking only applies to data-rich classes. For the rare classes, every generator scores close to zero. Thus, this evaluation cannot rank generators for the classes that motivated the generation of images in the first place (\Cref{sec:results_per_class_structure}).

\paragraph{Question $3$: Training-Data Composition}
Half of a small real training set can be replaced by generated images, but not all of it. With the same training budget, a class trained on a combination of real and generated images is as accurate as a class trained on real images only for two of the three models. The third model loses slightly (\Cref{tab:band-grid-real}). A class trained on generated images alone achieves only about half the accuracy of a class trained on real photographs. This finding aligns with an earlier result indicating that a classifier trained on generated images alone requires several times more images to match one trained on real photographs \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023}. Both comparisons are based on single runs, and the bands contain different species, so the results are directional. The generator comparison, which was designed independently, reaches the same conclusion. It can rank generators for classes with real photographs but not for classes without them (\Cref{sec:results_band_granularity}). Generated images expand a limited real pool. They do not replace it.

Accuracy measured on the mixed test set does not translate to real photographs. Every model scores higher on the mixed test set and almost every class scores higher on its synthetic test images than on real ones (\Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta}). This is partly due to difficulty rather than domain shift because a synthetic test image contains one centered animal, whereas a real photograph often contains several. The discrepancy is largest where the training images were synthetic. Both test domains rank the models the same way, so the conclusions of this chapter hold on the real photographs alone.

The band grid also shows where accuracy is lost. The detectors find almost every animal. However, they lose accuracy when identifying the species. Most of these errors occur between look-alike groups rather than within them (\Cref{tab:granularity-gaps}). Within the groups, confusion is common but concentrated in a few pairs, and it mostly runs in one direction. For example, the Asian elephant is usually labeled as an African elephant, but rarely the other way around (\Cref{sec:results_lookalike_confusion}). A nano-scale detector loses its accuracy on this taxonomy by naming the species, not by finding the animal. A detector that could fall back to a coarser name would retain much of the lost accuracy (\Cref{sec:further_work}).

\paragraph{Question $4$: Latency and Model Size Without Compression}
Without compression, the nano-scale detector takes less than half a second per frame on the proxy CPU, which is about half the time required by \textit{YOLOv5s} (\Cref{tab:embedded-latency}). The two ensembles are one to two orders of magnitude slower. The teacher ensemble takes tens of seconds per frame, while the pairing of \textit{YOLOv5s} with the same classifier takes several seconds. The nano-scale detector has a small fraction of their parameters and loses only a small part of the teacher's accuracy, which is itself an upper bound (\Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}). These figures were measured at full precision and bound the latency from above. The accelerated integer path of the target remains unmeasured. These measurements confirm the argument of \Cref{sec:lit_md_speciesnet}. A two-stage ensemble of this size does not fit on a mobile system-on-chip. A single detector trained on the full taxonomy is two orders of magnitude smaller and faster, with only a slight loss in accuracy.

Both the size of the gain and its source were unexpected. \textit{YOLO26n} is the smallest, most accurate, and fastest measured detector. It is much smaller than \textit{YOLOv5s} and is clearly more accurate, even though they were both trained on the same data with the same schedule. Two generations of progress within the same model family outweighed anything that tuning the older architecture could plausibly have returned. The same pattern is visible across the field at present, with large language models as the most visible example. Adopting the latest iteration often yields better results than investing the same effort in the previous one. This is a general observation, not a measured result of this work. The strongest reason to repeat such comparisons whenever a new generation appears is the measured part: a newer nano-scale model beating a larger, older one on every axis.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The answers above are limited as follows. Each limit is stated alongside its consequence.

\paragraph{A specific use case}
There is no comparable body of published results regarding the fine-grained detection of $225$ mammal species in handheld photographs, and there is no benchmark against which the figures here can be compared. Therefore, the findings are ordinal within this dataset. A $225$-class species mAP cannot be compared to a published $80$-class COCO figure (\Cref{sec:mixed_default}). Whether the same methods can be applied to a different taxonomy, sensor, or class balance is untested. Whether these findings can be generalized beyond this dataset is a question that remains unanswered.

\paragraph{Limited data}
No curated dataset exists for this task of the kind that autonomous driving research can draw on. The corpus was assembled from openly licensed photographs, excluding some sources. An audit revealed that the implemented allow-list included imagery beyond the stated policy (\Cref{sec:sourcing_execution}). Therefore, every model was trained and evaluated on a corpus that a stricter policy would have reduced further. Rare species remained rare after sourcing. This shortage limits both training and evaluation. $17$ of the $50$ Band A classes have fewer than $30$ real test images, and the smallest class has six. Their figures are limited by the test set before they are limited by any model. Each model contributes one training run without confidence intervals. \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows a $0.03$ gap reversal between seeds, so differences of that size carry no information in this work.

\paragraph{Generation cost}
The production synthetic corpus of $23{,}850$ images cost approximately $\$785$ at list price (\Cref{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}). This budget determined how many images each class received. The vendor of the detector family trained here recommends a minimum of $1{,}500$ images and $10{,}000$ labeled instances per class \cite{TipsBestYOLOv52026}. This work trained on $200$ images per class in Bands A to C and a maximum of $1{,}500$ images in Band D, with only Band D reaching the lower end of the recommended amount. The supplemented bands are about an eighth of that amount. Increasing the number of images for every class to $1{,}500$ would require roughly $337{,}500$ images, about fourteen times the size of the generated corpus, as well as a correspondingly larger budget.  % 225 * 1,500 = 337,500 images, 337,500 / 23,850 = 14.2 times the corpus, at $785 / 23,850 = $0.033 per image about $11,100
Open-weight models would eliminate the per-image price but not the gap. Even with their best prompt, they fall measurably short of API services in classes where small details are critical (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). For a task that depends on fine markings, the volume of training data is also a cost factor.

\paragraph{Pretraining overlap}
All of the models here started with pretrained weights: the students from COCO and the teacher from a large camera-trap corpus. Each of the nine COCO animal categories that correspond to this taxonomy, from domestic cat to giraffe, has a counterpart in Band D (\Cref{tab:appendix-class-band-assignment-d}). Other well-photographed Band D species may also appear in the pretraining corpora, which are not fully documented. Therefore, part of the gap between Band D and the other bands could come from pretraining rather than the training composition. The COCO overlap is a quantifiable indication of this possibility. The question is not established here.

\paragraph{Distillation in its weakest form}
The distillation tested here only transfers the teacher's final class distribution, the least informative form examined in \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation}. A confident teacher's distribution is close to a one-hot label. Two properties of this setting further weaken it. First, the ground-truth species labels were produced by the same classifier pipeline that later became the teacher (\Cref{sec:speciesnet_matching,sec:teacher_finetuning}). The soft targets therefore largely repeat the hard labels and deviate from them mostly where the teacher is incorrect. Second, a species label has one correct answer per animal. In domains where a prediction has no single correct outcome, such as predicting the behavior of other road users while driving, a teacher's distribution over plausible outcomes provides more information than a hard label can. A strong teacher can be expected to help a student much more in such domains than in this one. Only one configuration and one pairing were run, so stronger forms of distillation remain untested for this task.

\paragraph{Model selection}
Two detector architectures were trained, and the starting situation chose both. The detector already in use fixed the family, and its smallest current member became the student (\Cref{sec:model_selection}). Due to time constraints, nano-scale detectors outside that family were set aside, so neither student won a benchmarked comparison, and no hyperparameter search was run on either. A broader set of architectures and a tuning study would likely improve the accuracy and deployment of the results. This would also demonstrate how size and accuracy relate at this scale, which the two data points cannot show.

\paragraph{Teacher figures are upper bounds}
The teacher ensemble localizes with the same detector that produced the ground-truth boxes. Therefore, its figures and every teacher-to-student gap are upper bounds (\Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison}). Its fine-tuning gain carries a second caveat, because part of the fine-tuning corpus was admitted by agreeing with the classifier before fine-tuning (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}).

\section{Further Work}\label{sec:further_work}

These limits yield four directions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Re-examination as generators improve.} The generator ranking is a snapshot. Both tiers of generators improve within months, with the open-weight tier improving the fastest. A local model that reaches the current API level would eliminate the per-image pricing that restricts the volume of synthetic data. The \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability} comparison can be rerun with the same classes and prompts, and its conclusions should be verified whenever the generators change.
    \item \textbf{A broader model study.} Training nano-scale detectors from different families using the same data and protocol, along with a hyperparameter search, would demonstrate the extent to which the difference between the two students is due to architecture and the relationship between size and accuracy at this scale.
    \item \textbf{A curated mammal detection benchmark.} A licensed, species-level dataset with a fixed real test set would let future work on this task compare results at all. This work's corpus, curation pipeline, and evaluation protocol are a starting point for one.
    \item \textbf{A coarse fallback in the single detector.} The dominant error is naming a species from the wrong look-alike group. A hierarchical label space or loss that falls back to genus or family below a confidence threshold would transform these errors into coarse-correct answers. The classifier ensemble of \Cref{sec:lit_md_speciesnet} exhibits this behavior, which the single detector lacks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This work asked how a detector small enough for embedded hardware should be trained to recognize $225$ mammal species when real photographs are scarce for many of them. An automatically curated corpus of openly licensed photographs was assembled. Twelve generator and prompt-regime cells were compared on the same classes. Four detectors were trained and evaluated using real and generated test images. The runtime of each detector was measured on a device adjacent to the target.

Generated images can replace half of a small, real training set with no measurable loss. However, a class trained on generated images alone reaches only about half the accuracy of a class trained on real photographs. Synthetic data therefore extends a small real pool and does not replace one. The amount of detail provided to the generator is more important than the generator itself, and neither apparent realism nor price predicts the usefulness of its output. A current nano-scale detector trained directly on the data is smaller, more accurate, and faster than the previous generation's larger detector. Distilling a far larger fine-tuned model into it does not improve upon direct training. It loses its accuracy in naming the species once the animal is found.

The contribution is a measured map of where generated imagery helps a fine-grained wildlife detector, and evidence that a nano-scale single-stage detector resolves a $225$-species taxonomy at a fraction of the size and time of the two-stage ensemble it was compared against. The speed at which the field is advancing is evident in the data itself. The generated zebra images in \Cref{fig:appendix-gallery-plains-zebra} exhibit stripe patterns that are indistinguishable by eye from those in the photographs in \Cref{fig:zebra-species}. A few years ago, no generator could produce this level of detail for a single species, and supplementing a fine-grained species dataset with generated images was not feasible. Generators and detectors are improving at a pace that may overtake these findings within a few years, so the value of this work lies as much in a protocol that can be rerun as in the numbers it reports today.
