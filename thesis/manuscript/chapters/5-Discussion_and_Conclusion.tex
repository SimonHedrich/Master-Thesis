\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Every accuracy figure in this chapter is measured on the real test set, which \Cref{sec:mixed_default} designates the primary-evaluation figure and which orders the models the same way as the mixed test set does.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does knowledge distillation from a fine-tuned teacher model into a nano-scale student model yield higher detection accuracy than fine-tuning the same student directly on the wildlife dataset, given the domain shift from COCO-style classes to $225$ mammal species?
    \item Which synthetic-image generator produces the most useful training data for this domain, and do the two criteria available before any detector is trained, apparent visual realism and per-image price, predict that ranking?
    \item How does the composition of a class's training data, from synthetic images alone through a large pool of real photographs, affect detection accuracy, and does accuracy measured on the mixed test set hold up on the real test set alone?
    \item How far is a nano-scale detector from real-time inference without quantization or any other compression step, and how much latency and model size does it save against the two-stage ensembles, measured on a target-adjacent proxy device?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Question $1$: Distillation Against Direct Fine-Tuning}
Direct fine-tuning is the better recipe for this student. It leads the distilled student in all $17$ cells of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, at a mAP of $0.529$ against $0.479$ over all bands. The margin is widest on Band A, at $0.300$ against $0.239$, where the teacher's soft targets were expected to help most because the supervision there is synthetic and thin. The teacher's class distribution added nothing to the training signal that the labels did not already carry, and the Band A margin suggests that where it deviated from them, it did harm. \Cref{sec:limitations} argues why this pairing and this form of distillation could contribute so little. The comparison ran one response-based configuration, at $T = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$, with one teacher at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$. The two runs differed in effective batch size (\Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}). It rules out a benefit from that configuration and does not rule out knowledge distillation for this domain.

\paragraph{Question $2$: Generator Choice}
\texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest quality tier, prompted under the unabridged \texttt{full} regime, produced the most useful training data of the twelve cells, at $0.134 \pm 0.014$ mAP (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Neither of the two criteria available before a detector is trained predicted that ranking. The generator selected for the production dataset on apparent realism ranks third and trails the leader by roughly $28\%$ under the same prompt regime, and the two \texttt{gpt-image-2} tiers, priced $2.9$ times apart, are separated by $0.008$ mAP. What does predict the outcome is how much the generator is told. Compressing the prompt roughly halves downstream accuracy for both models tested, at $-45\%$ and $-48\%$ (\Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}), a larger effect than most differences between generators. Earlier work found the same, in that bare class-name prompts yield poor training data and alignment with the target classes, not visual fidelity, is the variable that matters \cite{aziziSyntheticDataDiffusion2023}. The ranking holds for the data-rich classes only. On the six rare classes every cell stays below $0.05$ average precision, so the executed evaluation axis cannot order generators for the classes that motivated generating images (\Cref{sec:results_per_class_structure}).

A qualitative observation belongs beside these measurements. The stripe patterns in the generated zebra images of \Cref{fig:appendix-gallery-plains-zebra}, set against the photographed animals of \Cref{fig:zebra-species}, reach a level of detail that the author could not distinguish from photographs by eye. This was not measured, since the blind rating rubric was not run, but it marks how far image generation has moved. Species detail of this kind was out of reach of generators a few years ago, and the present open-weight models appear to stand roughly where the proprietary services stood then. What is measured is the remaining gap, since under each model's best prompt no local model reaches any API service (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Supplementing a fine-grained species dataset with generated images has become feasible only recently, and it depends on that progress continuing.

\paragraph{Question $3$: Training-Data Composition}
Half of a small real training set can be replaced by generated images without measurable loss, and the whole of it cannot. At the matched budget of $200$ images per class, Band B trains on $100$ real and $100$ synthetic images and Band C on $200$ real ones. The two bands score $0.588$ against $0.591$ for \textit{YOLO26n} and $0.638$ against $0.639$ for the fine-tuned teacher, with \textit{YOLOv5s} the exception at $0.341$ against $0.386$ (\Cref{tab:band-grid-real}). Band A, trained on synthetic images alone, reaches $0.300$ for the primary detector, roughly half of what the same budget of real photographs achieves. That shape agrees with the finding that a classifier trained on generated images alone needs about five times as many of them to match real images of the same classes \cite{heSyntheticDataGenerative2023}. Both comparisons rest on single runs over bands that hold different species, so they are directional. The generator comparison, designed independently, reaches the same boundary, since it ranks generators for classes with real photographs and cannot for classes without them (\Cref{sec:results_band_granularity}). Generated images extend a small real pool and do not replace one.

The mixed test set overestimates accuracy on real photographs. For the primary detector, the reported value is $0.599$, whereas the measured value on real images is $0.529$. The paired per-class delta is negative for $208$ of the $224$ banded classes (\Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta}). Part of the difference is difficulty rather than domain shift, since a synthetic test image holds one centered animal while a real image holds $1.44$ on average, and the rest is largest exactly where training was synthetic. Both domains order the models the same way, which is why this chapter reports the real figure alone.

Where the remaining accuracy is lost is the second learning of the band grid. The primary detector recovers $0.798$ of the animals at $100$ detections against a mAP of $0.529$ (\Cref{tab:headline-coco12}). For every model, the drop from class-agnostic detection to naming a look-alike group is $16$ to $22$ times the further drop to naming the species within it (\Cref{tab:granularity-gaps}, computed on the mixed test set like the confusion counts below). The errors cross look-alike groups instead of staying inside them. Within the groups, confusion is common but concentrated, with $19.3\%$ of correctly grouped detections misnamed and five of the $30$ groups carrying $60\%$ of those errors, and it runs one way, as with the $1{,}545$ Asian elephants labeled African against $9$ in the reverse direction (\Cref{sec:results_lookalike_confusion}). Naming the species, not finding the animal, is where a nano-scale detector loses its accuracy on this taxonomy, and a detector that could fall back to a coarser name would keep much of what is lost (\Cref{sec:further_work}).

\paragraph{Question $4$: Latency and Model Size Without Compression}
The nano-scale detector runs in $423$\,ms per frame on the proxy CPU at full precision, against $949$\,ms for \textit{YOLOv5s} (\Cref{tab:embedded-latency}). The two ensembles set the scale. Composed estimates put the teacher ensemble at roughly $35$\,s per frame and the pairing of \textit{YOLOv5s} with the same classifier at roughly $5.4$\,s, so \textit{YOLO26n} is $83$ and $13$ times faster with $71$ and $22$ times fewer parameters, at $0.070$ mAP below a teacher figure that is itself an upper bound (\Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}). No compression pass was applied, so these figures bound the latency from above, and the accelerated integer path of the target remains unmeasured. The measurement confirms the argument of \Cref{sec:lit_md_speciesnet}: a two-stage ensemble of this size does not fit a mobile system-on-chip, and a single detector trained on the full taxonomy reaches two orders of magnitude less latency and size at a small loss of accuracy.

The magnitude of the gain and its source were both unexpected. \textit{YOLO26n} is the smallest, most accurate, and fastest detector measured. It is $2.8$ times smaller than \textit{YOLOv5s} and has a $0.122$ mAP advantage, even though both were trained on the same data under the same schedule. Two generations of architectural progress within the same model family outweighed anything that tuning the older architecture could plausibly have returned. The same pattern is visible across the field at present, with large language models as the most visible case, where adopting the latest iteration often yields better results than investing the same effort in the previous one. This is a general observation, not a measured result of this work. However, the measured part, a newer nano-scale model outperforming a larger, older model on every axis, is the strongest reason to repeat such comparisons as new generations appear.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

The answers above are contained within the boundaries below, each with its own consequence.

\paragraph{A specific use case}
There is no comparable body of published results for fine-grained detection of $225$ mammal species in handheld photographs, nor is there a benchmark against which the figures here could be compared. Therefore, the findings are ordinal within this dataset. A $225$-class species mAP cannot be read against a published $80$-class COCO figure (\Cref{sec:mixed_default}), and whether the same recipes transfer to another taxonomy, another sensor, or a different balance of classes is untested. Whether the findings generalize beyond this dataset is a question this work leaves open.

\paragraph{Limited data}
No curated dataset exists for this task of the kind that autonomous driving research can draw on. The corpus was assembled from openly licensed photographs, which excluded some sources. An audit of \Cref{sec:sourcing_execution} found that the implemented allow-list admitted imagery outside the stated policy. Thus, every model was trained and evaluated on a corpus that a stricter policy would have reduced further. Rare species remained rare after sourcing. The shortage bounds evaluation as much as training. $17$ of the $50$ Band A classes have fewer than $30$ real test images, and the smallest has six, so their figures are limited by the test set before they are limited by any model. Every model contributes one training run without confidence intervals, and \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows a gap of $0.03$ reversing between seeds, so differences of that size in this work carry no information.

\paragraph{Generation cost}
The production synthetic corpus of $23{,}850$ images cost approximately $\$785$ at list price (\Cref{sec:synthetic_generation_pipeline}). That budget determined how many images each class received. The vendor of the detector family trained here recommends a minimum of $1{,}500$ images and $10{,}000$ labeled instances per class \cite{TipsBestYOLOv52026}. However, this work trained on only $200$ images per class in Bands A to C and a maximum of $1{,}500$ images in Band D. Thus, only Band D reaches the lower end of the recommended amount, while the supplemented bands are only about an eighth of that amount. Increasing the number of images for every class to $1{,}500$ would require roughly $337{,}500$ images, about fourteen times the size of the generated corpus, and a correspondingly larger budget.  % 225 * 1,500 = 337,500 images, 337,500 / 23,850 = 14.2 times the corpus, at $785 / 23,850 = $0.033 per image about $11,100
Open-weight models would eliminate the per-image price, but not the gap. Even with their best prompt, they fall measurably short of API services in classes where small details are critical (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Therefore, for a task that depends on fine markings, the volume of training data is also a matter of cost.

\paragraph{Pretraining overlap}
Every model here started from pretrained weights, the students from COCO and the teacher from a large camera-trap corpus. Each of the nine COCO animal categories that map onto this taxonomy, from domestic cat to giraffe, has a counterpart in Band D (\Cref{tab:appendix-class-band-assignment-d}), and other well-photographed Band D species may appear in those pretraining corpora as well, which are not fully documented. Part of the gap between Band D and the other bands could therefore come from pretraining rather than from the training composition. The COCO overlap is a quantifiable indication of that possibility, and the question is not established here.

\paragraph{Distillation in its weakest form}
The distillation tested here only transfers the teacher's final class distribution, which is the least informative form surveyed in \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation}. Furthermore, a confident teacher's distribution is close to a one-hot label. Two properties of this setting further weaken it. First, the ground-truth species labels were produced by the same classifier pipeline that later became the teacher (\Cref{sec:speciesnet_matching,sec:teacher_finetuning}). Thus, the soft targets largely repeat the hard labels and deviate from them mostly where the teacher is incorrect. Additionally, a species label has one correct answer per animal. In domains where a prediction has no single correct outcome, such as predicting the behavior of other road users while driving, a teacher's distribution over plausible outcomes provides information that a hard label cannot. A strong teacher can be expected to help a student much more in such domains than in this one. Only one configuration and one pairing were run, so a stronger form of distillation on this task remains untested.

\paragraph{Model selection}
Two detector architectures were trained, and the starting situation chose both. The detector already in use fixed the family, and its smallest current member became the student (\Cref{sec:model_selection}). Nano-scale detectors outside that family were set aside for time, so neither student won a benchmarked comparison, and no hyperparameter search was run on either. A broader set of architectures and a tuning study would likely improve both the accuracy and the deployment side of the results, and would show how size and accuracy relate at this scale, which two points cannot.

\paragraph{Teacher figures are upper bounds}
The teacher ensemble localizes with the same detector that produced the ground-truth boxes, so its figures and every teacher-to-student gap are upper bounds (\Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison}). Its fine-tuning gain carries a second caveat, since part of the fine-tuning corpus was admitted by agreeing with the pre-fine-tuning classifier (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}).

\section{Further Work}\label{sec:further_work}

These limits yield four directions.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Re-examination as generators improve.} The generator ranking is a snapshot. Both tiers of generators improve within months. The open-weight tier improves fastest. A local model that reaches the current API level would remove the per-image price that bounds the volume of synthetic data. The comparison of \Cref{sec:results_synthetic_data_practicability} can be rerun on the same classes and prompts, and its conclusions should be retaken whenever the generators change.
    \item \textbf{A broader model study.} Training nano-scale detectors from other families on the same data under the same protocol, together with a hyperparameter search, would demonstrate the extent to which the gap between the two students is due to architecture and how size relates to accuracy at this scale.
    \item \textbf{A curated mammal detection benchmark.} A licensed, species-level dataset with a fixed real test set would let work on this task compare results at all. The corpus, the curation pipeline and the evaluation protocol of this work are a starting point for one.
    \item \textbf{A coarse fallback in the single detector.} The dominant error is a species named from the wrong look-alike group. A hierarchical label space or loss that falls back to genus or family below a confidence threshold would turn such errors into coarse-correct answers, which is the behavior the classifier ensemble of \Cref{sec:lit_md_speciesnet} has and the single detector lacks.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This work asked how a detector small enough for embedded hardware should be trained to recognize $225$ mammal species when real photographs are scarce for many of them. An automatically curated corpus of openly licensed photographs was assembled. Twelve generator and prompt-regime cells were compared on the same classes. Four detectors were trained and evaluated on real and generated test images. The runtime of each detector was measured on a device adjacent to the target.

Generated images can replace half of a small, real training set without measurable loss. However, a class trained on generated images alone achieves approximately half the accuracy of a class trained on real photographs. Synthetic data therefore extends a small, real pool and does not replace one. The amount of detail provided to a generator matters more than the generator chosen, and neither apparent realism nor price predicts the usefulness of its output. A current nano-scale detector trained directly on the data is smaller, more accurate, and faster than the previous generation's larger detector. Distilling a far larger fine-tuned model into it does not improve on direct training. It also loses its accuracy in naming the species once the animal is found.

The contribution is a measured map of where generated imagery helps a fine-grained wildlife detector, and evidence that a nano-scale single-stage detector resolves a $225$-species taxonomy at a fraction of the size and time of the two-stage ensemble it was compared against. Generators and detectors are improving at a pace that may overtake these findings within a few years, so the value of this work lies as much in a protocol that can be rerun as in the numbers it reports today.
