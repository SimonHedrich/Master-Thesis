\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{chapter:discussion_conclusion}

This chapter answers the four research questions of \Cref{sec:objective} from the evidence of \Cref{chapter:results}, states the limitations that bound each answer (\Cref{sec:limitations}), derives the further work they motivate (\Cref{sec:further_work}), and closes with what the work establishes (\Cref{sec:conclusion}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The research questions are restated verbatim and answered one at a time.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does knowledge distillation from a fine-tuned teacher model into a nano-scale student model yield higher detection accuracy than fine-tuning the same student directly on the wildlife dataset, given the domain shift from COCO-style classes to $225$ mammal species?
    \item Which synthetic-image generator produces the most useful training data for this domain, and do the two criteria available before any detector is trained, apparent visual realism and per-image price, predict that ranking?
    \item How does the composition of a class's training data, from synthetic images alone through a large pool of real photographs, affect detection accuracy, and does accuracy measured on the mixed test set hold up on the real test set alone?
    \item Does a nano-scale detector meet the per-frame latency budget of the target hardware without quantization or any other compression step, measured on a target-adjacent proxy device?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Question $1$: Distillation Against Direct Fine-Tuning}
No, at the one design point tested. Direct fine-tuning is ahead in all $17$ cells of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, reaching $0.599$ mAP on the mixed test set against the distilled student's $0.560$, and $0.529$ against $0.479$ on the real-only breakout. The margin is widest on Band A, whose supervision is entirely synthetic and thin, so the negative answer holds on the hypothesis's own best case rather than only on aggregate.

Three properties limit how far that answer generalizes. The distilled run trained at an effective batch size of $16$ against $32$ and ended early with its validation metric still rising, so batch size is a second varied factor and the magnitude of the gap is unquantified (\Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}). One $(T, \alpha)$ point ran rather than the grid of \Cref{sec:kd_loss}, at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$, which \Cref{sec:kd_loss} identifies as far outside the range treated as safe and as a documented setting for negative transfer. And the domain shift the question names as its premise, though present by construction, is unmeasured, because the student's zero-shot baseline was never produced (\Cref{sec:results_headline_mixed}). The comparison rules out a benefit from response-based distillation at $T = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$ with this pairing, and not knowledge distillation for this domain.

\paragraph{Question $2$: Generator Choice}
\texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest quality tier, under the unabridged \texttt{full} prompt regime, produced the most useful training data of the twelve cells compared, at $0.134 \pm 0.014$ mAP on the real test set (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Neither criterion the question names predicts that ranking. Apparent visual realism selected the incumbent, which ranks third of twelve, is beaten by roughly $28\%$ under an identical prompt regime and is tied with its own vendor's cheaper variant. Per-image price predicts nothing, since the two \texttt{gpt-image-2} tiers differ by a factor of $2.9$ in price and are separated by $0.008$ mAP against a combined standard deviation of roughly $0.018$, and in neither vendor family nor in the local grid is the cheaper cell the worse one (\Cref{sec:results_cost_vs_quality}).

A third criterion the question did not anticipate outranks both. Compressing the prompt roughly halves downstream accuracy, at $-45\%$ and $-48\%$ for the two models of \Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}, larger than most model-to-model differences in the ranking, so how much diagnostic detail a generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. That holds for data-rich classes and under the single evaluation axis executed. On the rare classes it answers nothing, since five of the six stay below $0.05$ average precision for every cell and one generator draws kinkajous with a ringed tail at no measured penalty (\Cref{sec:results_per_class_structure}). The comparison ranks generators for well-photographed classes and establishes that its own instrument cannot rank them for the long-tail classes that motivated generating images in the first place.

\paragraph{Question $3$: Training-Data Composition}
Composition matters non-linearly. Replacing half of a small real training set with synthetic imagery changes accuracy by no more than $0.003$ mAP in two of the three models, at $0.588$ against $0.591$ on the real test set for \textit{YOLO26n} and $0.638$ against $0.639$ for the fine-tuned teacher (\Cref{tab:band-grid-real}). \textit{YOLOv5s} is the exception, at $0.341$ against $0.386$. Replacing all of it leaves the primary detector at $0.300$, roughly half the $0.591$ the same budget of real photographs achieves. That gap is an order of magnitude larger than the Band B against Band C difference beside it, so its direction survives the absence of confidence intervals where the smaller difference does not. Each figure comes from a single run, and the bands hold different species assigned by data availability rather than at random.

Accuracy measured on the mixed test set does not hold up on the real test set alone. The real-only breakout falls below the mixed headline for all four models, by between $0.042$ and $0.089$ mAP (\Cref{tab:headline-cross-model}). The paired per-class delta shows why. $\Delta_{\text{domain}}$ is negative for $208$ of the $224$ banded classes and for all $76$ of Band A and Band B, and the synthetic test set is easier by a median of $0.21$ mAP on Band D and $0.72$ on Band A (\Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta}). Roughly $0.2$ mAP of that is not domain shift, since a synthetic test image holds one centered animal by construction while a real one averages $1.44$ boxes.

The watchdog \Cref{sec:mixed_default} attached to the mixed default therefore fired, and \Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} records the verdict. The designed revision, restoring the real test set as the reported default, was not carried out within the available time, which is a deviation from the stated protocol rather than an unexplored axis. Every headline figure in \Cref{chapter:results} appears with its real-only breakout beside it, which limits the damage without repairing it.

\paragraph{Question $4$: The Per-Frame Latency Budget Without Compression}
No configuration measured in \Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark} meets the project's $\leq 30$\,ms per-frame design target, and the shortfall is not marginal. The fastest model is \textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning, at $423$\,ms end-to-end on the \textit{Raspberry Pi 400} proxy, roughly $360$--$381$\,ms on the \textit{QCS605} CPU and about twelve times the budget, while \textit{YOLOv5s} needs $949$\,ms. The memory axis is met, with all three detectors below the $\leq 500$\,MB ceiling and neither teacher component under it. The teacher's latency settles a separate question, since \textit{MegaDetector v5a} alone takes $31.0$\,s per frame and the ensemble estimate reaches roughly $35$\,s, confirming by measurement what \Cref{sec:lit_yolo_family} argued: the ensemble cannot ship on this hardware.

This answer is not a verdict on the hardware. The question asks about a detector without quantization or any other compression step, and that omission is a scope decision rather than an attempt that failed, so the figures bound latency from above. The Hexagon 685 DSP has no analogue on the proxy, and the accelerated path a shipping product would use, for which \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference} reports order-of-magnitude gains from $8$-bit quantization, remains unmeasured. Whether $30$\,ms is reachable on the target is therefore still open.

\paragraph{Summary}
The two questions that received the bulk of the experimental effort returned usable findings, one negative about the criteria a practitioner reaches for first when choosing a generator and one positive about a bounded use of synthetic imagery. The two answered at a single design point returned clean negatives inside that scope. No accuracy target was set for this work, and none of the answers identifies accuracy as the blocking axis. The one measured obstacle to deployment is a compression problem.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Every answer above is bounded by the limitations below, each stated with its consequence.

\paragraph{No confidence intervals anywhere}
The bootstrap resampling specified in \Cref{sec:metric_selection} was never run, and every model contributes a single training run, so no difference reported in \Cref{chapter:results} is statistically significant. A direction holding across $17$ partly independent views of one pair of runs, or a gap the size of the one separating Band A from Band C, is unlikely to be seed noise. A difference of $0.003$ between Band B and Band C carries no information about which composition is better, and the seed episode of \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows a gap in that range reversing outright once two further seeds were added.

\paragraph{The watchdog revision was not carried out}
This limitation, alone in this section, deviates from a stated protocol rather than leaving an axis unexplored. \Cref{sec:mixed_default} committed in advance to revising the default evaluation axes if a systematic discrepancy appeared. It appeared, it is large and one-directional, and the revision did not follow. Every mixed-set figure in this work should be read as a ranking instrument and not as an estimate of accuracy on real photographs, with the real-only breakout beside it carrying that second reading.

\paragraph{Question $1$ rests on one cell of a large space}
The answer is bounded by four gaps. An evaluation of \textit{YOLO26n} from raw COCO weights failed on a missing data directory and was not retried, so every student figure is an absolute level rather than a gain over an untuned start. Only the default $(T, \alpha)$ point ran, so \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct} is one cell of the grid \Cref{sec:kd_loss} describes. One pairing was tested, and feature-based alignment was excluded on architectural grounds. And the multi-animal subset was never evaluated, although an image holding several animals is where a teacher's class distribution carries the most information hard targets do not, so the one mechanism that could have favored distillation was never probed. Reading Question $1$'s answer as a result about knowledge distillation for this domain would overstate it by the size of the space \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation} surveys.

\paragraph{No quantization-aware training}
No quantization, pruning or compression pass was applied, and \Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded} records that as a scope decision rather than an attempted step. The full-precision figures of \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} bound latency from above, particularly since the accelerated path on the \textit{QCS605} is a quantized one. Question $4$'s answer is a feasibility statement about unoptimized models, and calling it a deployment verdict would require that pass.

\paragraph{Every teacher figure is an upper bound}
The teacher ensemble localizes with the same detector that produced the ground-truth boxes of both test domains, so every teacher figure in \Cref{chapter:results} is an upper bound and the teacher-to-student gap with it (\Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison}). Its fine-tuning gain of $0.176$ mixed and $0.154$ real carries a second caveat, since part of that corpus was admitted by agreeing with the pre-fine-tuning classifier (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}).

\paragraph{The generator comparison supports ordinal claims only}
Six limits apply beyond the deferred evaluation axes (\Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict}). The annotations are automatic and un-reviewed. Seed counts are uneven, at three per API cell against two per local cell. The design is not a full factorial, evaluation is real-only, and three fairness asymmetries remain in the prompt handling. One planned cell was never generated, so the grid has no upper anchor. \Cref{tab:generator-ranking} therefore supports the ordinal claims of Question $2$ and neither an absolute accuracy figure nor a full crossing of generator against prompt length.

\paragraph{The blind rating rubric was never run}
The multi-rater rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} was not carried out, nor were three of the four automatic proxies, and the one computed discriminates too weakly to separate the local cells. The rubric was the designed instrument for the setting in which downstream accuracy has no resolution, which is the rare classes, so its absence is why a visually obvious species error appears in \Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples} and is detected by no executed measure.

\paragraph{Error types are not separated}
The error-type decomposition named in \Cref{sec:metric_selection} was left as a future add-on, so cross-group misclassification, duplicate detections and background false positives all land in $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ together (\Cref{sec:results_granularity_gaps}), which makes it an upper bound and not a measurement. The finding that classification rather than localization limits accuracy survives this, because the recall figures of \Cref{tab:headline-coco12} support it independently. No claim about which classification error dominates does.

\paragraph{Training cost is not comparable}
The wall-clock cost reported in \Cref{sec:results_general_observations} is not a throughput comparison, since every run overlapped with at least one other.

\section{Further Work}\label{sec:further_work}

Six follow-ons come out of those limits, ordered by cost-to-value ratio.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantization.} The largest remaining lever is a compression pass, since the accelerated path on the \textit{QCS605} is a quantized one and the figures of \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} miss the budget by roughly twelve times. Post-training quantization is the cheaper first step and quantization-aware training narrows the residual gap, both mapping onto the vendor toolchain named in \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference}. The measurement that settles Question $4$ has to be taken on the Hexagon 685 DSP itself. Reducing the input resolution is an independent second lever, and it needs a resolution-native training arm beside it.
    \item \textbf{Retroactive confidence intervals.} Bootstrap resampling over the stored per-image predictions would give every comparison the significance test it lacks, without retraining anything. The suite already accepts a resample count and the artifacts are archived, so the cost is compute rather than redesign. The comparisons that need it most are Band B against Band C and the two tied generator tiers.
    \item \textbf{Re-scoring the archived predictions.} Evaluating \textit{YOLO26n} from raw COCO weights is one run and the cheapest missing measurement here, turning every student figure from an absolute level into a gain. Re-scoring Setup A against Setup B on the images holding more than one animal is the concrete test of distillation's expected supervision advantage, and both prediction sets are archived, so it needs no retraining.
    \item \textbf{A wider distillation design space.} Question $1$'s answer is scoped to one cell, so the direct follow-on is the sweep over temperature $T$ and blend weight $\alpha$ that \Cref{sec:kd_loss} was written around. Two further axes matter more: closing the parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$ with an intermediate-capacity teacher assistant, which is the remedy \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation} reports for this configuration, and attention-aware detection distillation. Any repeat should hold the effective batch size equal across the two setups, which the comparison reported here did not.
    \item \textbf{The blind rating rubric.} The rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} remains fully specified and directly executable on the archived images, and it needs raters rather than compute. It is the axis that would resolve the rare-class question the executed axis has no power over.
    \item \textbf{The watchdog revision.} Restoring the real test set as the reported default is outstanding, and nothing blocks it but the rewriting effort, since every table already holds both domains. Redesigning the mixed condition is the larger version of that task, because the synthetic test set holds one centered animal per image and is easier for that reason alone.
\end{enumerate}

The first item decides whether the system is deployable at all. The rest decide how well it would perform once it is.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This work set out to build, train and evaluate a nano-scale wildlife species detector for embedded deployment, over a class universe of $225$ mammal species of which $50$ retain fewer than $150$ usable real photographs each. The substantive contribution sits in two sub-studies, while the distillation and deployment questions were each answered at a single design point.

The first is a controlled comparison of twelve generator and prompt-regime cells, each supplying $1{,}200$ synthetic training images of the same $12$ classes to the same detector under the same schedule. Neither criterion available before a detector is trained predicts what it achieves. The generator chosen for the production dataset on apparent realism ranks third of twelve, and per-image price is unrelated to outcome in both vendor families and the local grid. Prompt detail outranks generator identity, since compressing the prompt roughly halves downstream accuracy while the spread across generators under their best prompt is smaller. For the long-tail classes that motivated generating images at all, the comparison establishes that its own instrument has no resolution.

The second is the band comparison at a matched $200$-image training budget. Replacing half of a small real training set with synthetic imagery produces no measurable loss for the primary detector or the fine-tuned teacher, at $0.588$ against $0.591$ mAP on the real test set, and a loss of $0.045$ for \textit{YOLOv5s}. Replacing all of it roughly halves accuracy, at $0.300$ against $0.591$. Both rest on single runs and on bands that hold different species, so they are directional rather than exact. The paired per-class domain-shift delta then fired the watchdog attached to the mixed default, and the designed revision did not follow within the available time, which is the one protocol deviation this work discloses.

On the central question, distillation from the fine-tuned teacher lost to direct fine-tuning in all $17$ reported cells, at $0.599$ against $0.560$ mAP on the mixed test set and $0.529$ against $0.479$ on the real one. That is a negative result at one $(T, \alpha)$ point, with one pairing at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$, and three disclosed asymmetries keep it from being a clean single-factor comparison. It is nonetheless useful to anyone weighing the same architecture-scale gap, because the simpler recipe won on every axis measured, by the widest margin on the thin-data band where the teacher's soft targets were expected to help most.

\textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning is at once the smallest, the most accurate and the fastest detector measured here, at $2.71\,\mathrm{M}$ parameters, $0.599$ mAP on the mixed test set with $0.529$ on the real test set, and $423$\,ms per frame on the proxy device. That last figure is roughly twelve times the target hardware's per-frame budget, and no full-precision model measured here comes closer. No accuracy target was set for this work, and nothing in these measurements identifies accuracy as the obstacle to deployment. What measurably stands between this detector and a shippable model is a compression pass, and the accelerated path such a pass would target has not been measured at all.
