\chapter{Discussion and Conclusion}\label{chapter:discussion_conclusion}

This chapter answers the four research questions of \Cref{sec:objective} from the evidence of \Cref{chapter:results}, states the limitations that bound each answer (\Cref{sec:limitations}), derives the further work they motivate (\Cref{sec:further_work}), and closes with what the work establishes (\Cref{sec:conclusion}).

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The research questions are restated verbatim and answered one at a time.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does knowledge distillation from a fine-tuned teacher model into a nano-scale student model yield higher detection accuracy than fine-tuning the same student directly on the wildlife dataset, given the domain shift from COCO-style classes to $225$ mammal species?
    \item Which synthetic-image generator produces the most useful training data for this domain, and do the two criteria available before any detector is trained, apparent visual realism and per-image price, predict that ranking?
    \item How does the composition of a class's training data, from synthetic images alone through a large pool of real photographs, affect detection accuracy, and does accuracy measured on the mixed test set hold up on the real test set alone?
    \item How far is a nano-scale detector from real-time inference without quantization or any other compression step, and how much latency and model size does it save against the two-stage ensembles, measured on a target-adjacent proxy device?
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Question $1$: Distillation Against Direct Fine-Tuning}
No, at the one design point tested. Direct fine-tuning outperforms the distilled student in all $17$ cells of \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct}, achieving a mAP of $0.599$ on the mixed test set compared to the distilled student's $0.560$. On the real-only breakout, direct fine-tuning achieves a mAP of $0.529$ compared to the distilled student's $0.479$. The margin is widest on Band A, whose supervision is entirely synthetic and thin, so the negative answer holds on the hypothesis's own best case rather than only on aggregate.

Three properties limit how far that answer generalizes. The distilled run trained at an effective batch size of $16$ against $32$ and ended early with its validation metric still rising, so batch size is a second varied factor and the magnitude of the gap is unquantified (\Cref{sec:results_kd_basic_comparison}). One $(T, \alpha)$ point ran rather than the grid of \Cref{sec:kd_loss}, at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$, which \Cref{sec:kd_loss} identifies as far outside the range treated as safe and as a documented setting for negative transfer. And the domain shift the question names as its premise, though present by construction, is unmeasured, because the student's zero-shot baseline was never produced (\Cref{sec:results_headline_mixed}). The comparison rules out a benefit from response-based distillation at $T = 4$ and $\alpha = 0.5$ with this pairing, and not knowledge distillation for this domain.

\paragraph{Question $2$: Generator Choice}
\texttt{gpt-image-2} at its cheapest quality tier, under the unabridged \texttt{full} prompt regime, produced the most useful training data of the twelve cells compared, at $0.134 \pm 0.014$ mAP on the real test set (\Cref{tab:generator-ranking}). Neither of the two criteria named in the question predicts that ranking. Apparent visual realism selected the incumbent, which ranks third out of twelve. It is beaten by roughly $28\%$ under an identical prompt regime and tied with its own vendor's cheaper variant. Price per image predicts nothing since the two \texttt{gpt-image-2} tiers differ in price by a factor of $2.9$ and are separated by $0.008$ mAP, with a combined standard deviation of approximately $0.018$. In neither vendor family nor the local grid is the cheaper cell worse (\Cref{sec:results_cost_vs_quality}).

A third criterion the question did not anticipate outranks both. Compressing the prompt roughly halves downstream accuracy, at $-45\%$ and $-48\%$ for the two models of \Cref{tab:prompt-length-ablation}, larger than most model-to-model differences in the ranking, so how much diagnostic detail a generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. That holds for data-rich classes and under the single evaluation axis executed. On the rare classes it answers nothing, since five of the six stay below $0.05$ average precision for every cell and one generator draws kinkajous with a ringed tail at no measured penalty (\Cref{sec:results_per_class_structure}). The comparison ranks generators for well-photographed classes and establishes that its own instrument cannot rank them for the long-tail classes that motivated generating images in the first place.

\paragraph{Question $3$: Training-Data Composition}
Composition matters non-linearly. Replacing half of a small real training set with synthetic images reduces accuracy by no more than $0.003$ mAP in two of the three models. For \textit{YOLO26n}, it drops from $0.591$ to $0.588$ on the real test set, and for the fine-tuned teacher, it drops from $0.639$ to $0.638$ (\Cref{tab:band-grid-real}). \textit{YOLOv5s} is the exception, at $0.341$ against $0.386$. Replacing all of it leaves the primary detector at $0.300$, roughly half the $0.591$ the same budget of real photographs achieves. That gap is an order of magnitude larger than the difference between Bands B and C, so its direction survives the absence of confidence intervals, unlike the smaller difference. Each figure comes from a single run, and the bands contain different species that are assigned based on data availability rather than randomly.

Accuracy measured on the mixed test set does not translate to the real test set. For all four models, the real-only breakout falls below the mixed headline by between $0.042$ and $0.089$ mAP (\Cref{tab:headline-cross-model}). The paired per-class delta shows why. $\Delta_{\text{domain}}$ is negative for $208$ of the $224$ banded classes, as well as for all $76$ of Bands A and B. The synthetic test set is easier by a median of $0.21$ mAP for Band D and $0.72$ for Band A (\Cref{tab:domain-shift-delta}). About $0.2$ mAP of that difference is not due to domain shift since a synthetic test image contains one animal in the center by design, whereas a real image averages $1.44$ boxes.

The \Cref{sec:mixed_default} watchdog, which is attached to the mixed default, therefore fired, and \Cref{sec:results_domain_shift} records the verdict. The designed revision, which would have restored the real test set as the reported default, was not carried out within the available time. This deviation from the stated protocol is not an unexplored axis. Every headline figure in \Cref{chapter:results} appears with its real-only breakout beside it, limiting the damage but not repairing it.

\paragraph{Question $4$: Latency and Model Size Without Compression}
Real-time inference is not reached without further optimization, and no fixed latency requirement existed to pass or fail. The fastest model is \textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning, at $423$\,ms end-to-end on the \textit{Raspberry Pi 400} proxy and roughly $360$--$381$\,ms on the \textit{QCS605} CPU, while \textit{YOLOv5s} needs $949$\,ms. All three detectors stay below $400$\,MB of resident memory, and neither teacher component stays below $500$\,MB. The comparison with the two ensembles is the substantive answer (\Cref{sec:results_embedded_benchmark}). Against derived estimates of roughly $35$\,s for the teacher ensemble and $5.4$\,s for the production pairing of \textit{YOLOv5s} and \textit{SpeciesNet}, the nano-scale detector is $83$ and $13$ times faster with $71$ and $22$ times fewer parameters. It gives up $0.064$ mAP on the mixed test set and $0.070$ on the real one against the teacher ensemble, whose figure is itself an upper bound (\Cref{sec:limitations}). The measurement confirms what \Cref{sec:lit_yolo_vs_ensemble} argued: the teacher ensemble cannot ship on this hardware, and the nano-scale detector delivers a large gain in latency and size at a cost in accuracy that is small by comparison.

This answer is not a verdict on the hardware. The question asks about a detector without quantization or any other compression step, and that omission is a scope decision rather than an attempt that failed, so the figures bound latency from above. The Hexagon 685 DSP has no analogue on the proxy, and the accelerated path a shipping product would use, for which \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference} reports order-of-magnitude gains from $8$-bit quantization, remains unmeasured. How much of the remaining distance to real-time inference that path closes is therefore still open.

\paragraph{Summary}
The two questions that received the most experimental attention yielded usable results: one negative finding regarding the criteria a practitioner reaches for first when choosing a generator, and one positive finding regarding a bounded use of synthetic imagery. The two questions answered at a single design point yielded clean negatives within that scope. No accuracy target was set for this work, and none of the answers identifies accuracy as the limiting factor. What separates the measured latency from real-time use is the compression pass that was not carried out.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Each of the answers above is limited by the constraints below, each of which is stated with its consequence.

\paragraph{No confidence intervals anywhere}
The bootstrap resampling specified in \Cref{sec:metric_selection} was never run, and each model contributes only one training run. Therefore, no difference reported in \Cref{chapter:results} is statistically significant. A consistent trend across $17$ partly independent views of one pair of runs, or a gap the size of that between Bands A and C, is unlikely to be due to random variation. A difference of $0.003$ between Band B and Band C carries no information about which composition is better, and the seed episode of \Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict} shows a gap in that range reversing outright once two further seeds were added.

\paragraph{The watchdog revision was not carried out}
This limitation, alone in this section, deviates from a stated protocol rather than leaving an axis unexplored. \Cref{sec:mixed_default} committed to revising the default evaluation axes in advance if a systematic discrepancy appeared. It appeared, it is large and one-directional, and the revision did not follow. Every mixed-set figure in this work should be read as a ranking instrument, not an estimate of the accuracy of real photographs. The real-only breakout beside it carries that second reading.

\paragraph{Question $1$ rests on one cell of a large space}
The answer is bounded by four gaps. An evaluation of \textit{YOLO26n} from raw COCO weights failed due to a missing data directory and was not retried. Therefore, every student figure represents an absolute level rather than a gain over an untuned start. Only the default $(T, \alpha)$ point was run, so \Cref{tab:kd-vs-direct} is one cell in the \Cref{sec:kd_loss} grid. One pairing was tested, and feature-based alignment was excluded on architectural grounds. And the multi-animal subset was never evaluated, although an image holding several animals is where a teacher's class distribution carries the most information hard targets do not, so the one mechanism that could have favored distillation was never probed. Interpreting the answer to Question $1$ as a result regarding knowledge distillation in this domain would be an overstatement by the size of the space that \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation} surveys.

\paragraph{No quantization-aware training}
No quantization, pruning, or compression passes were applied, and \Cref{sec:results_kd_and_embedded} recorded this as a scope decision rather than an attempted step. The full-precision figures of \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} bound the latency from above, especially since the accelerated path on the \textit{QCS605} is quantized. The answer to Question $4$ is a feasibility statement about unoptimized models. Calling it a deployment verdict would require that pass.

\paragraph{Every teacher figure is an upper bound}
The teacher ensemble localizes with the same detector that produced the ground-truth boxes of both test domains, so every teacher figure in \Cref{chapter:results} is an upper bound and the teacher-to-student gap with it (\Cref{sec:results_regime_comparison}). Its fine-tuning gain of $0.176$ mixed and $0.154$ real carries a second caveat, since part of that corpus was admitted by agreeing with the pre-fine-tuning classifier (\Cref{sec:teacher_finetuning}).

\paragraph{The generator comparison supports ordinal claims only}
Six limits apply beyond the deferred evaluation axes (\Cref{sec:results_practicability_verdict}). The annotations are automatic and unreviewed. Seed counts are uneven, at three per API cell against two per local cell. The design is not a full factorial, evaluation is real-only, and three fairness asymmetries remain in the prompt handling. One planned cell was never generated, so the grid has no upper anchor. Therefore, \Cref{tab:generator-ranking} supports the ordinal claims of Question $2$, but not an absolute accuracy figure or a full crossing of generator against prompt length.

\paragraph{The blind rating rubric was never run}
The \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} multi-rater rubric was not carried out, nor were three of the four automatic proxies. The one that was computed discriminates too weakly to separate the local cells. The rubric was the designed instrument for the setting in which downstream accuracy has no resolution, which is the rare classes, so its absence is why a visually obvious species error appears in \Cref{fig:kinkajou-generator-samples} and is detected by no executed measure.

\paragraph{Error types are not separated}
The error-type decomposition named in \Cref{sec:metric_selection} was left as a future add-on, so cross-group misclassification, duplicate detections and background false positives all land in $\Delta_{\text{coarse}}$ together (\Cref{sec:results_granularity_gaps}), which makes it an upper bound and not a measurement. The finding that classification rather than localization limits accuracy remains true because the recall figures of \Cref{tab:headline-coco12} independently support it. No claim about which classification error dominates does.

\paragraph{Training cost is not comparable}
The reported wall-clock cost in \Cref{sec:results_general_observations} is not a throughput comparison because every run overlapped with at least one other.

\section{Further Work}\label{sec:further_work}

Six follow-ons emerge from these limits, ordered by cost-to-value ratio.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Quantization.} The largest remaining lever is a compression pass, since the accelerated path on the \textit{QCS605} is a quantized one and the full-precision figures of \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} remain far from real-time use. Post-training quantization is the cheaper first step and quantization-aware training narrows the residual gap, both mapping onto the vendor toolchain named in \Cref{sec:lit_embedded_inference}. The measurement that answers Question $4$ must be taken on the Hexagon 685 DSP itself. Reducing the input resolution is an independent second lever, and it needs a resolution-native training arm beside it.
    \item \textbf{Retroactive confidence intervals.} Bootstrap resampling over the stored per-image predictions would give every comparison the significance test it lacks, without retraining anything. The suite already accepts a resample count and the artifacts are archived, so the cost is compute rather than redesign. The comparisons that need it most are Band B against Band C and the two tied generator tiers.
    \item \textbf{Re-scoring the archived predictions.} Evaluating \textit{YOLO26n} from raw COCO weights requires only one run and is the cheapest missing measurement here, turning every student figure from an absolute level into a gain. Re-scoring Setup A against Setup B on images with more than one animal tests the expected advantage of distillation's supervision, and both prediction sets are archived, so no retraining is necessary.
    \item \textbf{A wider distillation design space.} Question $1$'s answer is scoped to one cell, so the direct follow-on is the sweep over temperature $T$ and blend weight $\alpha$ that \Cref{sec:kd_loss} was written around. Two further axes matter more: closing the parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$ with an intermediate-capacity teacher assistant, which is the remedy \Cref{sec:lit_knowledge_distillation} reports for this configuration, and attention-aware detection distillation. Any repeat should hold the effective batch size equal across the two setups, which the comparison reported here did not.
    \item \textbf{The blind rating rubric.} The rubric of \Cref{sec:generator_comparison_evaluation} remains fully specified and directly executable on the archived images, and it needs raters rather than compute. It is the axis that would resolve the rare-class question the executed axis has no power over.
    \item \textbf{The watchdog revision.} Restoring the real test set as the reported default is outstanding, and nothing blocks it but the rewriting effort, since every table already holds both domains. Redesigning the mixed condition is the larger version of that task, because the synthetic test set holds one centered animal per image and is easier for that reason alone.
\end{enumerate}

The first item decides whether the system is deployable at all. The rest decide how well it would perform once it is.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This work set out to build, train and evaluate a nano-scale detector for wildlife species recognition on embedded hardware, over a class universe of $225$ mammal species of which $50$ retain fewer than $150$ usable real photographs each. The substantive contribution sits in two sub-studies, while the distillation and deployment questions were each answered at a single design point.

The first is a controlled comparison of twelve generator and prompt-regime cells. Each cell supplies $1{,}200$ synthetic training images of the same $12$ classes to the same detector under the same schedule. Neither criterion available before a detector is trained predicts what it achieves. The generator selected for the production dataset based on apparent realism ranks third out of twelve, and per-image price is unrelated to the outcome in both vendor families and the local grid. Prompt detail outranks generator identity since compressing the prompt roughly halves downstream accuracy, whereas the spread across generators under their best prompt is smaller. For the long-tail classes that motivated the generation of images in the first place, the comparison shows that the instrument itself has no resolution.

The second is the band comparison at a matched $200$-image training budget. Replacing half of a small real training set with synthetic images results in no measurable loss for the primary detector or the fine-tuned teacher, at $0.588$ against $0.591$ mAP on the real test set, and a loss of $0.045$ for \textit{YOLOv5s}. Replacing all of the real training set with synthetic imagery roughly halves the accuracy to $0.300$ against $0.591$. Both rest on single runs and bands containing different species, making them directional rather than exact. The paired per-class domain-shift delta then triggered the watchdog attached to the mixed default. The designed revision did not occur within the available time, which is the one protocol deviation this work discloses.

Regarding the central question, distillation from the fine-tuned teacher performed worse than direct fine-tuning in all $17$ reported cells. The results were $0.599$ versus $0.560$ mAP on the mixed test set and $0.529$ versus $0.479$ on the real one. This is a negative result at a single $(T, \alpha)$ point with one pairing at a parameter ratio of roughly $70$ to $1$. Three disclosed asymmetries prevent a clean single-factor comparison. Nevertheless, it is useful for those considering the same architecture-scale gap because the simpler recipe won on every measured axis, with the widest margin on the thin-data band where the teacher's soft targets were expected to help the most.

\textit{YOLO26n} under direct fine-tuning is at once the smallest, the most accurate and the fastest detector measured here, at $2.71\,\mathrm{M}$ parameters, $0.599$ mAP on the mixed test set with $0.529$ on the real test set, and $423$\,ms per frame on the proxy device. That figure is roughly one thirteenth of the derived latency of the production pairing of \textit{YOLOv5s} and \textit{SpeciesNet} and one eighty-third of the teacher ensemble, at a cost of $0.064$ mAP against the teacher on the mixed test set. Real-time inference on the device still requires further optimization, and nothing in these measurements identifies accuracy as the obstacle to deployment. What measurably stands between this detector and a shippable model is a compression pass, and the accelerated path such a pass would target has not been measured at all.
