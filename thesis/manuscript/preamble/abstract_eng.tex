In wildlife monitoring, recognizing an animal's species automatically is difficult when the recognition must run on a battery-powered device carried into the field. Such a device cannot host the large models that perform best on this task, and photographs are plentiful for a few familiar animals and scarce for most others. This thesis examines how a compact species detector should be trained under those two constraints, and what the available data allows.

A training dataset is assembled from openly licensed photographs and curated automatically. Species that remain poorly represented are supplemented with generated images, and several image generators are compared beforehand to find which produces the most useful training material. The compact detector is then trained twice, once by distilling a much larger fine-tuned model into it and once directly on the same data, and the speed of both is measured on hardware comparable to the intended device.

The comparison finds no advantage for distillation over direct training, least of all for the species whose training data is thinnest. Neither the apparent realism of the generated images nor their price predicts how useful they prove. How much descriptive detail the generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. Replacing part of a small set of real photographs with generated images proves harmless, while replacing all of them does not. Accuracy measured on generated test images overstates accuracy on real ones, a discrepancy this work reports rather than corrects. The resulting detector misses the intended device's speed budget by an order of magnitude, since no compression step was applied yet, and closing that gap is left to future work.
