In wildlife monitoring, automatically recognizing an animal's species poses a significant challenge, particularly when the recognition must run on a battery-powered device carried into the field. Such devices cannot host the large models that perform best on this task, so the model must answer within a fixed time budget for every frame. The subject matter compounds the difficulty. Many species differ only in fine markings rather than in shape or context, and photographs are plentiful for a few familiar animals and scarce for most others. This thesis addresses the problem of training a compact species detector for embedded use, by examining how such a model should be trained and what the available data allows.

The research begins with a review of object detection, generative image models and knowledge distillation, focusing on what each offers a model constrained by size. A training dataset is then assembled from openly licensed photographs and curated automatically, since the class list is far too large to inspect by hand. Because many species remain poorly represented even after this effort, generated images supplement them, and several image generators are first compared to find which produces the most useful training material. The compact detector is then trained in two ways, once by distilling a much larger fine-tuned model into it and once by training it directly on the same data, so that both approaches can be compared under matched conditions.

The models are evaluated on real and generated photographs alike, and their speed is measured on hardware comparable to the intended device. The comparison finds no advantage for distillation over training the compact model directly, and the shortfall is widest for the species whose training data is thinnest, where it had been expected to help most. Neither property a practitioner can judge before training, the apparent realism of the generated images or their price, predicts how useful they prove. How much descriptive detail the generator is given matters more than which generator is chosen. Replacing part of a small set of real photographs with generated ones proves harmless, while replacing all of them does not. Accuracy measured with generated test images is shown to overstate accuracy on real ones, a discrepancy this work reports rather than corrects. What finally separates the resulting detector from deployment is not its accuracy but its speed, which no compression step was applied to reduce. The findings offer guidance for further research and practice wherever a recognition model must be trained for a fine-grained domain and then fitted to a device far smaller than that domain usually demands.
