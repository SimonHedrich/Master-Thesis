In wildlife monitoring, automatically recognizing an animal's species is difficult when the recognition process must run on a battery-powered device that is carried into the field. Such a device cannot support the large models that perform best for this task. On the other hand, while there are a large number of photographs available for many animal species, only a limited number are freely accessible for some rare species. The model in use is an object detector that locates the animal in the frame and assigns a species to it. Both steps are demanding, but classifying the species is the harder one because many species differ only in fine markings. This thesis examines how a compact detector should be trained under those two constraints, and what the available data allows.

A training dataset is assembled from openly licensed photographs and automatically curated. Species that are poorly represented are supplemented with generated images. Several image generators are compared beforehand to determine which produces the most useful training material. The compact detector is then trained twice, once by distilling a much larger fine-tuned model into it and once directly on the same data. The speed of both processes is measured on hardware comparable to the intended device.

Distillation offers no advantage over direct training, and for the species with the scarcest training data the hoped-for gain fails to appear entirely. Neither the apparent realism of the generated images nor their price turns out to be a measure of their usefulness. What matters more than the choice of generator is how much detail the generator is given about the desired image. Generated images can replace part of a small set of real photographs without loss of accuracy, but not the whole set. Accuracy measured on generated test images is also more favorable than on real photographs. The trained detectors are many times faster and smaller than the models previously used for wildlife species recognition, at comparatively little cost in accuracy.
