\section{Per-Class Training-Band Assignment}\label{sec:appendix_band_assignment}

\Cref{sec:band_structure} defines the four training bands and states how many classes each holds, but names only a handful of member species per band by way of illustration. \Cref{tab:appendix-class-band-assignment-abc,tab:appendix-class-band-assignment-d} complete that listing, giving every one of the $225$ classes together with the band it was assigned to. The assignment is reproduced from the per-class evaluation export \texttt{eval\_per\_class.csv}, the artifact the evaluation suite itself scored against, rather than recomputed from the post-curation pool sizes. Recomputation would risk a table that disagrees with the one the trained models were actually built on. The band totals match \Cref{tab:band-structure} exactly, at $50$ classes in Band A, $26$ in Band B, $26$ in Band C and $122$ in Band D.

Two conventions govern how the two tables are read. Class names appear exactly as the dataset's own category list spells them, in lowercase and including the several genus-level and family-level labels that stand in for groups of species too fine to separate. Entries run column-wise, down the first class column, then down the second, then down the third, so that each band occupies a contiguous stretch. The final entry is the \texttt{human} class, which carries no band at all. \Cref{sec:band_structure} excludes it from the band structure because it is a negative and contextual class rather than a wildlife species, and the export records its band as \texttt{negative} accordingly.

\section{Look-Alike Group Listing}\label{sec:appendix_lookalike_groups}

\Cref{sec:lookalike_groups} establishes the frozen table that every coarse-granularity figure in this work is computed against, and reports its shape as $174$ groups of which $30$ hold more than one class. \Cref{tab:appendix-lookalike-groups} lists those $30$ groups in full, each with its members and with the origin of the grouping. The remaining $144$ groups are singletons, in which a class stands alone and a coarse judgment is therefore identical to a fine one. Group labels and memberships are reproduced from \texttt{lookalike\_groups\_v2.csv}, the version the evaluation suite loads. The governing criterion is the one stated in \Cref{sec:gt_annotation_integrity}, namely whether confusing two classes in a single still frame counts as a forgivable slip or as a real failure.

The \texttt{override} entries in the last column are where that criterion overrules taxonomy, and three of them carry most of the deviation from a pure genus rollup. The genus \textit{Panthera} was split three ways, isolating lion and tiger as singletons and keeping only the rosette-patterned leopard, jaguar and snow leopard together, since a tiger mistaken for a lion is not a subtle error. The genus \textit{Equus} was split into the three zebras and the three unstriped equids on the same reasoning about stripes. Grant's gazelle, Thomson's gazelle and springbok were merged across three genera into one \texttt{gazelle} group, reversing an earlier decision to keep them apart. Three cross-genus merges predate these and are retained unchanged, covering the two elephants, the lynx and caracal cluster, and the three hyaenas.

\section{Data Sourcing and Curation Details}\label{sec:appendix_data_details}

These are the filter specifications and controlled vocabularies that \Cref{sec:data_sourcing_and_taxonomy,sec:data_quality_and_curation} state only by their effect.

\subsection{COCO Filter for the Human Class}\label{sec:appendix_human_filter}

The \texttt{human} class was drawn from COCO 2017 under a filter written for it, since the class serves as a contextual negative in an outdoor observation scenario rather than as a portrait subject (\Cref{sec:sourcing_execution}). An image was excluded if it carried a vehicle or animal supercategory annotation, if it was flagged \texttt{iscrowd=1}, or if it contained more than three person annotations. A retained bounding box was required to occupy between $5\%$ and $60\%$ of the frame area, to sit at least $2\%$ of the frame away from the image edge, and to have a height-to-width ratio of at least $0.5$, which biases the class toward standing figures over incidental ones. Post-download quality checks additionally required a shorter side of at least $256\,\mathrm{px}$, a Laplacian blur variance of at least $100$, and a mean saturation of at least $15$, the last of these rejecting grayscale and infrared frames.  % scripts/download_coco_humans.py

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Wikimedia Category Exclusion Vocabulary}\label{sec:appendix_wikimedia_vocabulary}

The Wikimedia Commons crawl of \Cref{sec:sourcing_execution} selects categories through an explicit account of what a wildlife photograph is \emph{not}. A category is excluded if its subject is artwork or illustration, an anatomical specimen or isolated body part, a range map, philatelic material, tracks and spoor, taxidermy or a trophy, a food product, or a fossil. Categories in which the animal is incidental to the subject are excluded on the same grounds, for example \enquote{things named after lions} or \enquote{people with cheetahs}. Exclusions cascade to subcategories, on the reasoning that a Commons subcategory inherits its parent's framing, so every subdivision of an art category is still art. Partial-animal imagery such as heads, paws and trophy shots is therefore attacked at three independent points: in this vocabulary, in the caption-based rescue stage, and in the caption evaluation criteria of \Cref{sec:staged_filtering_pipeline}.

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Caption Generation and Evaluation Settings}\label{sec:appendix_caption_settings}

Captions for the quality evaluation of \Cref{sec:staged_filtering_pipeline} were generated with \textit{Florence-2-base} at batch size $6$, a $768\times768$ input resolution and greedy decoding. The rescue stage of the same section uses \textit{Florence-2-large} in detailed-captioning mode.

The instruction given to the language model that judges the resulting captions is as notable for what it forbids as for what it asks. It names, explicitly, several properties that are \emph{not} grounds for rejection: humans present in the frame, domesticated animals, blurred or dark backgrounds, and descriptions of the animal's color or condition. Each corresponds to a plausible failure in which a model asked to judge image quality from a caption would confidently discard valid training data, and the first would additionally have contradicted the deliberate inclusion of a human class.

\section{Training and Experiment Tracking}\label{sec:appendix_training_details}

How runs were recorded, supplementing \Cref{sec:model_training_experiment_tracking}.

\subsection{Experiment Tracking and Run Artifacts}\label{sec:appendix_experiment_tracking}

Runs are tracked on a self-hosted \textit{MLflow} \cite{mlflowPythonAPI2024} server. Each run logs its whole constant module in a single call, not a hand-picked subset, together with the commit hash, the device, the realized split sizes and the effective batch size. A finished run's configuration is therefore recoverable from the tracking record instead of from whatever the working tree happened to contain on the day it started, which matters in a repository where several training and generation efforts overlapped in time. Loss components and learning rate are recorded per step and per epoch, validation mAP per evaluation, and the augmentation configuration of \Cref{sec:augmentation_reproducibility} arrives inside the same parameter set. Checkpoints, the run log and the per-class results attach as run artifacts, and tags separate the distilled runs from the direct ones.

Every run also writes a directory on disk that mirrors that record, so a run stays interpretable when detached from the tracking server. The directory name is timestamped and encodes the distinguishing flags, and it holds the checkpoints, the run log and the evaluation report together. Per-class results are written as plain artifact files. The most recent checkpoint is refreshed every epoch rather than only on an improvement, so an interruption between improvements cannot discard several days of training. All tracking calls are wrapped so that a failure to log cannot abort a multi-day run, at the cost that a run can in principle finish with incomplete tracking.

\subsection{Export Path and Runtime Selection on the Proxy Device}\label{sec:appendix_embedded_export}

The export path runs from each training checkpoint to \textit{TorchScript} and to \textit{ONNX} at opset~$17$ with static batch-1 shapes. Because both detector pipelines bypass \textit{Ultralytics}' high-level interface and store bare parameter dictionaries, the exporter rebuilds each architecture through this project's own model factories before tracing. Every exported graph was then checked against its \textit{PyTorch} reference on the host before it reached the device, and all five reproduce it to within floating-point accumulation noise. The export stage freezes a hashed copy of each source checkpoint and builds the host reference from that copy, so a checkpoint rewritten by a still-running training job cannot reach the comparison.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/pi_results/, scripts/benchmark/1b-convert_and_verify.py

Two observations concern the runtime rather than the models. \textit{ONNX Runtime} was faster than \textit{TorchScript} for every model that ran under both, by factors of $1.16$ to $1.75$, and it was the only runtime that executed every configuration without aborting. Multi-threaded \textit{TorchScript} terminates with an illegal-instruction fault on \textit{YOLO26n} on this ARMv8.0 core, and disabling its oneDNN backend extended that failure to every model rather than removing it.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/torchscript_sigill_finding.md
Runtime selection on this class of hardware is therefore a correctness question before it is a performance one. Second, no NCNN or GPU figure could be produced: the device's Vulkan stack is functional, but the only available ONNX-to-NCNN converter fails on both detectors, so the \textit{VideoCore VI} was never reachable.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/ncnn_conversion_finding.md

\subsection{Thermal Validity of the Runtime Campaign}\label{sec:appendix_thermal_validity}

The measurements behind \Cref{tab:embedded-latency} are not thermally compromised. All $76$ cells were taken under the \texttt{performance} governor with a monitor sampling core temperature, frequency and the SoC throttle register throughout. None tripped the validity gate, the peak temperature reached was $64.8$\,\textdegree C against an $80$\,\textdegree C threshold, and the throttle register read zero for the entire campaign. Sustained runs of several hundred iterations drifted by between $+0.28\,\%$ and $+2.33\,\%$, which bounds the thermal effect on this passively cooled device well below the differences the table reports.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/thermal_summary.csv

\subsection{Host-Device Numerical Parity}\label{sec:appendix_host_device_parity}

Exporting the detectors and running them on a different instruction set does not change what they compute. Over a fixed $600$-image subset, the device reproduces between $98.97\,\%$ and $99.47\,\%$ of the host's detections at an intersection-over-union of $0.99$ or better, and the subset mAP differs by at most $0.0020$.
% reports/embedded_benchmark/parity_summary.json
That residual is best read against a same-machine control: host GPU and host CPU, with identical weights and identical code, already disagree on $0.15$--$0.31\,\%$ of detections, because floating-point accumulation order differs between kernels and a near-tie in score can reorder which box survives. One of the six comparisons falls marginally below the $99\,\%$ criterion set in advance, at $98.97\,\%$. It is reported as a miss rather than absorbed by relaxing the threshold after the fact, and its mAP delta stays inside tolerance.
